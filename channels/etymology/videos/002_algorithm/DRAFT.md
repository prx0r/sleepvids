# DRAFT — "The Word 'Algorithm'"
## Sleepy Etymology — Episode 2

# SECTION 1: THE HOOK, THE MAP, AND THE COZY OPENING

Today, "the algorithm" decides what you see, what you buy, who you meet. It's the invisible force behind every social media feed, every search result, every recommendation. You've probably blamed it today. You've probably thanked it today. You've probably cursed it today. And every time you said the word, you were saying a man's name.

The word algorithm comes from al-Khwarizmi. A Persian mathematician who lived in Baghdad in the ninth century. His name was mangled through Latin, corrupted by Greek, and became the word for the thing that now runs your life. A man became a method. A method became a system. A system became a demon.

But the word traveled far to get here. It crossed continents. It crossed languages. It crossed centuries. It was mangled by translators who didn't care about names. It was corrupted by scholars who thought it looked Greek. It was reshaped by a printing error that stuck. And somewhere along the way, the man disappeared. Only the word remained.

Donald Glover, accepting an award in 2018, said: "First, I want to thank the great algorithm that put us all here." He was joking. But he was also serious. The algorithm had put him there — had decided that his work should be seen, had decided that his art should reach millions. The algorithm had made him famous. And he was thanking it the way you thank a god.

Mohammed Shirwa, a forty-one-year-old Uber driver in Rotterdam, told The Guardian in 2026: "It is like someone watching you all the time and knowing about your weakness — the boss is the algorithm. All the time the algorithm is learning about you and what you are willing to accept. So the prices go low but you are stuck. It knows you need the job."

Two people. Two relationships with the same word. One grateful. One afraid. Both speaking to a ghost.

For the next two hours, we're going to follow this word on a journey. We'll begin in Uzbekistan, where a man was born near the Aral Sea in the eighth century. We'll travel to Baghdad, where he wrote a book that changed mathematics. We'll watch Latin translators mangle his name, French scribes soften it, English corruption twist it. We'll trace the word from a personal name to an abstract concept to an invisible force.

We'll read passages from his actual book — the opening lines, still readable after twelve hundred years. We'll read Donald Knuth calling Euclid's algorithm "the granddaddy of all algorithms." We'll read Knuth on the spelling corruption, calling it a "pseudo-etymological perversion." We'll read Deborah Gordon on how ants use algorithms that mirror the Internet. We'll follow the loop: person → method → system → demon.

We'll follow the word into the natural world — where ants solve mathematical problems without brains, where slime molds solve mazes, where evolution itself is an algorithm. We'll follow it into the competing terms — the war between algorists and abacists, the 1503 woodcut that shows the battle. We'll follow it into the "al-" family — alcohol, alkali, alchemy — all fossils of a civilization that preserved human knowledge while Europe slept.

And then we'll come back to where we started. To a man's name. Still in your mouth. Every time you say algorithm.

So settle in. Get comfortable. Close your eyes if you like. We have twelve centuries to cover. And it begins with a man from a city that no longer exists.

Our journey begins in the eighth century, in a city called Khwarazm — modern-day Khiva, Uzbekistan, near the Aral Sea. A man was born there. His name was Muhammad ibn Musa. And his family name — al-Khwarizmi — meant "the man from Khwarazm." In the ninth century, that was enough. You didn't need a surname that described what you did. # SECTION 2: THE MAN AND HIS BOOK

Al-Khwarizmi was born around 780 CE in Khwarazm, a region near the Aral Sea in what is now Uzbekistan. His family name — al-Khwarizmi — meant "the man from Khwarazm." It was a toponymic name, a name that told you where someone was from. In the ninth century, that was enough. You didn't need a surname that described what you did. You needed one that described where you came from.

The Library of Congress describes him this way: "Born into a Persian family in Khuwarizm, Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi was a Muslim mathematician, astronomer, and geographer, and a scholar in the famed House of Wisdom in Baghdad."

He was Persian. He was a mathematician, an astronomer, and a geographer. And he was invited to Baghdad, to the House of Wisdom — Bayt al-Hikma — the most advanced research center of its time. The House of Wisdom was founded by Caliph Harun al-Rashid and expanded by his son al-Ma'mun. It was a place where scholars from across the Islamic world gathered to translate Greek and Indian manuscripts, to study mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and philosophy. Al-Khwarizmi worked there between 813 and 833 CE.

The House of Wisdom was not just a library. It was an academy. Scholars came from Persia, India, Greece, Syria, Egypt. They translated the works of Euclid, Ptolemy, Aristotle. They studied Indian mathematics, Persian astronomy, Greek geometry. They synthesized. They built on what came before. And al-Khwarizmi was at the center of it.

At the House of Wisdom, al-Khwarizmi studied ancient Greek and Indian mathematics. He studied the Indian numeral system — the digits zero through nine — and he saw something that European mathematicians, still using Roman numerals, had not seen. He saw that these numerals, with their place values and their zero, made calculation possible in ways that Roman numerals never could. Try dividing MCCXLVII by XLII using Roman numerals. Then try it with 1247 and 42. The Hindu-Arabic system didn't just make calculation easier. It made new kinds of calculation possible.

Around 820 CE, he wrote his most famous book. Let me read you the opening lines. They are still readable after twelve hundred years. The book begins:

"When I considered what people generally want in calculating, I found that it always is a number. I also observed that every number is composed of units, and that any number may be divided into units. Moreover, I found that every number which may be expressed from one to ten, surpasses the preceding by one unit: afterwards the ten is doubled or tripled just as before the units were: thus arise twenty, thirty, etc. until a hundred: then the hundred is doubled and tripled in the same manner as the units and the tens, up to a thousand; so forth to the utmost limit of numeration."

Think about that. A man sat down in Baghdad in the year 820 and wrote those words. He was starting from first principles. He was explaining what numbers are. And he was right. Every number is composed of units. Every number may be divided into units. That's what numbers are. That's what they've always been. He was explaining something so fundamental that we forget it needs explaining.

The book was called Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa-l-muqabala. The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing. The Library of Congress describes it as "a useful work, with examples and applications for everyday life, in areas such as trade, legal inheritance, and surveying." This book established algebra as a separate field of mathematics. The word algebra itself comes from the title — from al-jabr, meaning "reunion of broken parts," the operation of adding a number to both sides of an equation to consolidate terms.

But he wrote another book too. Algoritmi de numero Indorum — The Book of Indian Numerals. This book introduced the Hindu-Arabic numeral system to the Western world. It taught European mathematicians how to use zero, how to use place value, how to calculate in ways that Roman numerals made impossible. The book was translated into Latin in the twelfth century, and the translators rendered al-Khwarizmi's name as "Algoritmi." The Latin manuscripts are commonly referred to by the first two words with which they start: Dixit algorizmi. So said al-Khwarizmi. So said the man from Khwarazm.

Here's the irony: al-Khwarizmi's algorithms were used for almost three centuries, until they were replaced by Al-Uqlidisi's methods that could be carried out with pen and paper. His methods became obsolete. But his name survived. The word outlived the work. The name became more permanent than the mathematics.

And the same man gave us two of the most important words in mathematics: algorithm and algebra. Both from the same person. Both from the same books. Both mangled through translation. One from his name, one from his book's title. The man from Khwarazm gave Western civilization the language of computation and the language of equations. And neither word sounds like a name.

But here's something to think about. Al-Khwarizmi didn't invent algorithms. He gave them his name. Algorithms existed long before him. Euclid described an algorithm for finding the greatest common divisor of two numbers around 300 BCE — twelve hundred years before al-Khwarizmi was born. Knuth called it "the granddaddy of all algorithms, because it is the oldest nontrivial algorithm that has survived to the present day." The concept of algorithm is older than the word. The practice came first. The name came later.

And the practice goes back even further than Euclid. An arxiv paper from 2026 traces algorithmic thinking to Pingāla, an ancient Indian scholar from around 200 BCE, who used binary representation in his studies of poetic meter. The paper suggests "not a single continuous line of transmission, but the repeated independent reappearance of related procedures in distinct contexts." Algorithms keep being invented, independently, by different cultures, because the problems they solve are universal. The need to follow step-by-step instructions to reach a goal is not a human invention. It's a feature of reality itself.

And this is where it gets truly strange. Algorithms exist in nature. Ants solve the traveling salesman problem — finding the shortest path between food sources — without any central control, using nothing but pheromone trails. Deborah Gordon, a biologist at Stanford, discovered that harvester ants use an algorithm remarkably similar to the Transmission Control Protocol that regulates data traffic on the Internet. Both systems use simple, local feedback to regulate activity. The ants were doing it first. Evolution produced the algorithm. Humans just named it.

Gordon calls it "the anternet." She points out that the algorithm harvester ants use to regulate foraging is similar to the TCP protocol that prevents data congestion on the Internet. Both systems ask: is there enough bandwidth? If yes, send more. If no, send less. The ants were solving the same problem computers solve, and they were doing it with pheromones instead of packets.

Slime molds solve mazes. They find the shortest path between food sources without any brain, without any nervous system, without anything we'd recognize as intelligence. They do it through chemistry — through chemical gradients that guide their growth toward food and away from danger. It's an algorithm. It's step-by-step. It's computation without a computer.

DNA replicates itself through algorithmic processes. The double helix unwinds. Enzymes read the base pairs. New strands are assembled. It's a step-by-step procedure that produces copies with astonishing accuracy. It's an algorithm written in chemistry.

And evolution itself can be understood as an algorithm — variation, selection, reproduction, a step-by-step procedure that produces complexity from simplicity. The word algorithm describes something that was happening long before anyone had a word for it. Al-Khwarizmi didn't invent the thing. He just gave us the name. The ants were doing it before him. The slime molds were doing it before him. DNA was doing it before him. He just wrote it down.

# SECTION 3: WHAT IS AN ALGORITHM?

Before we go further, let's make sure we know what we're talking about. What is an algorithm? The word sounds intimidating. It sounds like something only mathematicians and computer scientists understand. But it's not. You've been using algorithms your entire life.

An algorithm is a recipe. That's it. That's the whole idea.

Think about a recipe for grilled cheese. Butter two slices of bread. Put cheese between them. Heat a pan on medium. Cook the sandwich about three minutes per side. Take it off when both sides are golden. Follow those steps in that order, and you get a grilled cheese sandwich. Skip a step, or do them out of order, and you get something else. The recipe is the algorithm. The sandwich is the result.

Pedro Domingos, in his book The Master Algorithm, puts it simply: "An algorithm is a sequence of instructions telling a computer what to do." And as he explains, algorithms are reducible to three logical operations: AND, OR, and NOT. While these operations can chain together in extraordinarily complex ways, at core algorithms are built out of simple rational associations.

Knuth defined an algorithm in The Art of Computer Programming with five properties. It must have finiteness — it must always terminate after a finite number of steps. It must have definiteness — each step must be precisely defined. It must have input — zero or more quantities given before the algorithm begins. It must have output — at least one quantity produced. And it must have effectiveness — all operations must be sufficiently basic that they can, in principle, be done exactly and in finite length.

Knuth also wrote: "The distinguishing feature of an algorithm is that all vagueness must be eliminated; the rules must describe operations that are so simple and well defined they can be executed by machine." That's the key. An algorithm leaves nothing to imagination. A recipe says "add a pinch of salt" — a human cook knows what that means. An algorithm says "add one quarter teaspoon of salt" — because a computer will sit there confused, or dump the entire box, if you don't specify.

This is why algorithms can be powerful. Once you have written the steps correctly, the computer can repeat them a thousand times, perfectly, without getting tired or bored or distracted. Your grilled cheese recipe makes one sandwich. A computer following a recipe can do its job a million times before lunch.

And you don't need a computer to use this idea. Tying your shoes is an algorithm — a fixed set of steps that always gives the same result. The directions to a friend's house are an algorithm — turn left at the gas station, then it's the third house on the right. Your morning routine is an algorithm — the same steps, in the same order, every day.

The difference between a recipe and a computer algorithm is precision. A recipe can say "cook until done." An algorithm must say "cook until the internal temperature reaches 165 degrees Fahrenheit." A recipe can say "a handful of flour." An algorithm must say "two cups of all-purpose flour, sifted." The algorithm leaves nothing to chance. Every step is defined. Every outcome is predictable.

And this is what makes algorithms scary. Because when an algorithm decides what you see on your phone, it's not guessing. It's following steps. Precise, well-defined steps. Steps that were written by someone. Steps that have a purpose. The algorithm doesn't decide what to show you. The algorithm follows instructions. Someone else decided.

# SECTION 3: THE JOURNEY OF THE WORD

The word algorithm has traveled twelve centuries to reach your mouth. And every step of the way, it was changed.

It began as Arabic: al-Khwarizmi. A name. A toponym. "The man from Khwarazm." In the ninth century, in Baghdad, this was just a name — the name of a mathematician who wrote books about equations and numerals. The name carried no special weight. It told you where someone was from. Nothing more.

Then the books were translated. In the twelfth century, Latin translators rendered his name as Algoritmi. The Arabic definite article "al-" was preserved, but the rest was mangled. Al-Khwarizmi became Algoritmi. A Persian name, Latinized. The translators were scholars, but they were not careful with names. They were careful with mathematics. The numbers survived. The name did not.

The Latin manuscripts are commonly referred to by the first two words with which they start: Dixit algorizmi. So said al-Khwarizmi. Imagine that phrase being read aloud in a twelfth-century European scriptorium. A monk, copying a Latin translation of an Arabic book, writing "Dixit Algorizmi" at the top of the page. He was copying the name of a man who had been dead for four hundred years. He had no idea who Algoritmi was. He just knew the mathematics worked. He didn't care about the man. He cared about the numbers.

From Medieval Latin algorismus, the word traveled to Old French. In the thirteenth century, French scribes wrote it as algorisme. The Latin suffix "-us" became the French "-e." The word softened. It lost its sharpness. It became French. The French were not interested in the man. They were interested in the method. The method was called algorisme — the Arabic way of counting.

From Old French, it entered Middle English. Around 1300, English speakers said algorisme. It meant "the Arabic numeral system." The system of counting with zero and place value. The system that al-Khwarizmi had introduced. The word still carried his name, but barely. Most English speakers had no idea who Algoritmi was. They just knew the system worked.

For three hundred years, the word stayed as algorisme. And for those three hundred years, there was a war. Not a war with swords. A war with counting. On one side stood the algorists — mathematicians who used pen and paper, following al-Khwarizmi's methods, writing numbers in the Hindu-Arabic system. On the other side stood the abacists — mathematicians who used the abacus, counting with beads on wires, following the old Roman methods.

The war lasted from the twelfth century to the sixteenth. A woodcut from 1503, in Gregor Reisch's Margarita Philosophica, shows the allegory: Arithmetica, the personification of mathematics, instructing an algorist and an abacist. The algorist writes with pen and paper. The abacist counts on an abacus. There was keen competition between the two from the introduction of algebra into Europe in the twelfth century until its triumph in the sixteenth.

The algorists won. Pen and paper beat beads on wires. The Hindu-Arabic numeral system beat Roman numerals. And the word algorism — the word for the winning method — survived. The losers' word, abacus, survived too, but as a relic, a museum piece. The winner's word became the word for the future.

Fibonacci helped seal the victory. In 1202, he published Liber Abaci — the Book of Calculation — which introduced the Hindu-Arabic numeral system to European merchants. He didn't use the word algorism. He called it modus indorum — the method of the Indians. But the method was the same one al-Khwarizmi had described four centuries earlier. The man's name was forgotten. The man's method conquered Europe.

Then something happened. In the 1690s, English scholars were writing about mathematics. They used the word algorisme. But they noticed something: the word looked like it should be connected to Greek arithmos, meaning "number." Arithmetic came from arithmos. Algorisme looked like it should too. So they changed the spelling. They added a "-th-" where there had been an "-s-." Algorisme became algorithm.

The -th- in algorithm has nothing to do with Greek. It's a mistake. A corruption. A mangled transliteration of a Persian name, further mangled by a false connection to Greek. Knuth wrote: "Gradually the form and meaning of algorism became corrupted; as explained by the Oxford English Dictionary, the word 'passed through many pseudo-etymological perversions, including a recent algorithm, in which it is learnedly confused' with the Greek root of the word arithmetic." The word's spelling is a lie. It says "arithmos" but it means "Khwarazm." It says "number" but it means "a man from Uzbekistan."

The Oxford English Dictionary called the change a "pseudo-etymological perversion." That's a polite way of saying the spelling is wrong. Scholars who should have known better changed a Persian name to look Greek. They didn't understand the word's origin, so they imposed their own. The -th- is a scar. A mark of European arrogance on an Arabic word.

And the same family of words that gave us algorithm also gave us algebra. From the same man. From the same books. Al-Khwarizmi wrote Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa-l-muqabala. The word algebra comes from al-jabr — "reunion of broken parts." The operation of adding a number to both sides of an equation to consolidate terms. Two of the most important words in mathematics. Both from the same person. Both from the same books. Both mangled through translation. One from his name, one from his book's title.

NPR noted in 2026: "Other scientific words and names, many beginning with al — Arabic for 'the' — made their way into English during a flowering of Islamic science and math that began in the 8th century. Examples include words like alcohol, alkali, alchemy, and in astronomy, star names such as Altair, Alkaid, Alcor and Aldebaran." Algorithm is part of this family. It's not alone. It's one of dozens of Arabic words that English absorbed during the Islamic Golden Age. Each word is a fossil. A trace of a civilization that preserved and advanced human knowledge while Europe slept.

From the 1690s onward, the word algorithm meant "Arabic system of computation." But the meaning broadened. By the 1930s, it meant any method of computation. Any step-by-step procedure for solving a problem. Any recipe that took an input and produced an output. The word had traveled from a personal name to an abstract concept.

And then computing happened. In the 1950s, the computer language Algol was coined from "algo(rithmic) l(anguage)." The word algorithm became the word for what computers do. The step-by-step procedure. The recipe. The instruction set. Edsger Dijkstra developed what became Dijkstra's algorithm — a way of finding the shortest road distance between two points, key to computer-based mapping. Susan McRoy, chair of the Computer Science Department at the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, noted that "long before modern computers, algorithms proved extremely useful in various fields of mathematics, such as business, surveying and navigation." The word algorithm jumped from mathematics to engineering. It was no longer about numbers. It was about paths.

Then Donald Knuth published The Art of Computer Programming in the 1970s, a multi-volume treatise that made algorithms the foundation of computer science. Knuth wrote: "Therefore I wish to propose analysis of algorithms as an appropriate name for the subject matter covered in these books. This name is meant to imply 'the theory of the properties of particular computer algorithms.'" And: "An algorithm must be seen to be believed." And: "The best way to communicate from one human being to another is through story."

Bill Gates said: "If you think you're a really good programmer… read Knuth's Art of Computer Programming… You should definitely send me a résumé if you can read the whole thing." The New York Times called it "the profession's defining treatise." The word had found its home. Algorithm meant computing.

And then the internet happened. And social media. And recommendation engines. And "the algorithm" became the invisible force that decides what you see. The word didn't just describe computation anymore. It described control. A word that meant a step-by-step procedure now meant a step-by-step procedure that decides what you think.

The philosopher G.K. Chesterton once wrote: "There are no uninteresting things, only uninterested people." The algorithm has taken this literally. It watches what you're interested in. It learns your patterns. It adjusts. It gives you more of what you click on, less of what you don't. It creates a world tailored to your preferences. And then it sells that world back to you.

Donald Glover thanked it. Uber drivers cursed it. The Guardian called it "the boss." A word that meant a man from Uzbekistan now means the thing that controls your attention. The thing that decides what you see when you open your phone. The thing that knows you better than you know yourself.

# SECTION 4: ALGORITHMS IN THE WILD

An algorithm is everywhere. You just don't see it. Every time you open Spotify, an algorithm decides what you hear. Every time you search on Google, an algorithm decides what you find. Every time you open Instagram or TikTok, an algorithm decides what you see. Every time you use GPS, an algorithm finds the shortest route. Every time you sort a list, you're running an algorithm. Every time you tie your shoes, you're following an algorithm. The world runs on algorithms. It always has.

But here's what's interesting. Algorithms aren't just in computers. They're in nature. They're in art. They're in music. They're in the very fabric of reality. An algorithm is just a process — a step-by-step procedure that transforms an input into an output. And processes are everywhere.

Consider Conway's Game of Life. In 1970, the mathematician John Conway invented a game played on a grid of cells. Each cell is either alive or dead. The rules are simple: a dead cell with exactly three live neighbors becomes alive. A live cell with two or three live neighbors survives. Otherwise, the cell dies. That's it. Four rules. No brain. No consciousness. No intention.

And yet, from these four rules, extraordinary complexity emerges. Gliders appear — small patterns that move across the grid. Spaceships form — larger patterns that travel. Oscillators pulsate. Still lifes stabilize. And in 1982, it was proved that the Game of Life is Turing complete — meaning it can simulate any computation that any computer can make. From four rules, you can build a universal computer.

The Game of Life is an algorithm that creates life. Not biological life. But something that looks like life. Something that moves, grows, reproduces, dies. From four rules. From simplicity. From an algorithm.

And algorithms make art. Generative art — art created by algorithms — has been around since the 1960s. Artists write code. The code follows rules. The rules produce images. The images surprise even the artist. Philip Galanter defined generative art as "any art practice where the artist uses a system, such as a set of natural language rules, a computer program, a machine, or other procedural invention, which is set into motion with some degree of autonomy contributing to or resulting in a completed work of art."

Algorithms compose music. Procedural music generation creates sound through algorithms — rule-based systems that produce melodies, harmonies, rhythms. Video games use algorithmic music that adapts to the player's actions. The music changes depending on what you do. The algorithm listens, and responds.

And algorithms recommend. Spotify has six thousand two hundred and ninety-one microgenres. It sorts your listening habits into clusters — the stuff you listen to every day, the stuff that sounds like your stuff, and everything else. Netflix has eight hundred engineers working on its recommendation system. It tracks what you watch, when you watch, how long you watch, what device you use. It even tracks whether you pause, rewind, or abandon. Seventy-five percent of viewer activity on Netflix is driven by recommendation. The algorithm decides what you see.

TikTok's algorithm is the most sophisticated recommendation system on the internet. It assigns special attention to implicit signals — actions you carry out without being completely aware. Every second you spend looking at a video adds information to its system. That's why many people feel the application reads their mind. In reality, the system is analyzing extremely detailed patterns of behavior.

The word algorithm describes all of this. The ants. The slime molds. The Game of Life. The generative art. The Spotify playlist. The Netflix recommendation. The TikTok feed. The GPS route. All of it. All processes. All step-by-step procedures. All algorithms.

The word has traveled from a man's name to a description of reality itself. Because reality is algorithmic. Nature runs on algorithms. Evolution is an algorithm. DNA replication is an algorithm. The weather is an algorithm. The stock market is an algorithm. Everything that follows rules is an algorithm. And everything follows rules.

# SECTION 5: THE ALGORITHM THAT LEARNS

For most of human history, algorithms were written by people. A human decided the steps. A human wrote them down. A computer followed them. Input in, output out. No learning. No adaptation. Just execution.

But then something changed. In the 1940s, researchers began asking a question: could a machine learn? Could an algorithm write itself? Could a computer improve its own performance without a human telling it how?

The idea came from biology. The brain, researchers noticed, learns through interactions among neurons. In 1943, American neuroscientists Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts modeled biological neural networks using simple mathematical rules. They created artificial neurons — mathematical functions that could be connected together in networks, just like the neurons in a brain.

For decades, these neural networks were too small to do anything useful. They could recognize simple patterns. They could classify basic inputs. But they couldn't do anything that required understanding. They couldn't read. They couldn't see. They couldn't speak.

Then, in the 2010s, something happened. Computing power grew. Data grew. And neural networks grew. Deep learning — neural networks with many layers — began to work. Really work. They could recognize faces. They could translate languages. They could generate text. They could play games better than any human.

The Turing Award laureates Yoshua Bengio, Yann LeCun, and Geoffrey Hinton wrote in 2021: "Research on artificial neural networks was motivated by the observation that human intelligence emerges from highly parallel networks of relatively simple, non-linear neurons that learn by adjusting the strengths of their connections. This observation leads to a central computational question: How is it possible for networks of this general kind to learn the complicated internal representations that are required for difficult tasks such as recognizing objects or understanding language?"

The answer was depth. Shallow networks didn't work. But deep networks — networks with many layers of artificial neurons — could learn. They could learn to see. They could learn to hear. They could learn to speak. They could learn to write.

And then came the language models. GPT-3 with one hundred and seventy-five billion parameters. GPT-4 with even more. These models generate text that reads like a human wrote it. They answer questions. They write essays. They compose poetry. They are algorithms that learned to simulate intelligence.

Andrew Ng, one of the pioneers of deep learning, wrote: "The idea behind a neural network is simple: For any task, there's a function that can perform it. A neural network constitutes a trainable function by combining many simple functions, each executed by a single neuron." The key word is trainable. The algorithm learns. It improves. It gets better at its task by processing more data.
