
# SECTION 6: THE ALGORITHM AND YOU

Let's bring it back to you. Right now, in this moment, an algorithm is working on your behalf. Or against you. Depending on how you look at it.

When you open your phone in the morning, before you've had your coffee, before you've spoken to another human being, an algorithm has already decided what you'll see. It knows what you clicked on yesterday. It knows how long you lingered on each post. It knows what you skipped. It knows what you shared. It knows what you searched for at two in the morning when you couldn't sleep.

The algorithm doesn't know your name. It doesn't know your face. But it knows your behavior. And in the twenty-first century, behavior is everything. Every click is a signal. Every pause is a signal. Every scroll is a signal. The algorithm reads these signals like a book. And it writes the next chapter based on what it reads.

TikTok's algorithm is particularly revealing. El País reported in 2026: "In contrast to other social media platforms that take into account the pages we follow, showing us related content, TikTok is largely based on an 'interest graph,' attempting to determine what we like by observing our behavior, not just social connections." Every second you spend looking at a video adds information to its system. The algorithm doesn't just show content — it influences the way you discover information. When you interact repeatedly with videos about a specific topic, the system offers more related content. It creates a feedback loop. You watch what it shows you. It shows you more of what you watch.

Georgia Tech is studying this. Munmun De Choudhury is leading a four-year, one-point-seven million dollar study using data from more than ten thousand adolescent users to audit TikTok's recommendation algorithm and study its impact on young people's behavior and mental health. "We don't understand passive social media consumption very well," she said. "So we hope to close that gap and learn what that looks like."

The algorithm doesn't just recommend. It shapes. It nudges. It creates a world that fits your preferences. And then it sells that world back to you. Netflix knows it has ninety seconds to convince you it has something to watch before you abandon the service. So it personalizes. It tailors. It creates a homepage that feels like it was made just for you. Because, in a sense, it was.

The Guardian reported in 2026 that a journalist turned off her Instagram algorithm for twenty-four hours. She described the experience as traveling back to 2012 — before everything on the internet was personalized. The feed was random. It was chaotic. It was boring. Because without the algorithm, you see what everyone sees. And what everyone sees is not what you want to see.

The algorithm knows what you want to see. That's its job. And it's very, very good at it. So good that we've forgotten what the world looks like without it. So good that we've forgotten that someone wrote the rules. Someone decided what counts as engagement. Someone decided what counts as relevant. Someone decided what you should see.

The man from Khwarazm wrote a book about equations. Twelve centuries later, his name is the word for the thing that decides what you think about. The loop is complete. Person → method → system → demon. And the demon is still walking.
