
# SECTION 4: THE MODERN DEMON

The algorithm is al-Khwarizmi's ghost. It's his name, haunting every phone, every feed, every recommendation. Every time you say "the algorithm," you're saying his name. You're speaking Persian. You're speaking a language that hasn't been a living tongue for a thousand years. You're carrying a sound across centuries.

And there's another loop. Al-Khwarizmi introduced the concept of zero to the West. Zero is the foundation of binary. Binary is the foundation of computing. Computing is the foundation of AI. The man who gave us zero gave us the word for the thing that now uses zero to think. He gave the West the tool. And now the tool has outgrown the West. The tool has outgrown everyone. The tool runs on zero. The zero came from India. The method came from Baghdad. The name came from Uzbekistan. The computation happens in server farms in Virginia. And the result appears on your phone, in your feed, deciding what you see. The journey is complete. A man's name has become the architecture of attention.

The spelling is still wrong. The -th- in algorithm has nothing to do with Greek arithmos. It's a mistake. A corruption. A mangled transliteration of a Persian name, further mangled by a false connection to Greek. Knuth called it a "pseudo-etymological perversion." The word's spelling is a lie. It says "arithmos" but it means "Khwarazm." It says "number" but it means "a man from Uzbekistan."

Every time you say algorithm, you're saying a lie. And a truth. And a man's name. All at once. A man who sat in Baghdad in the ninth century, writing equations by lamplight, never knowing that his name would become the word for the thing that watches you while you sleep.

And algorithm is not alone. It's part of a family. The Arabic prefix "al-" means "the." And dozens of English words begin with it — words that arrived during the Islamic Golden Age, when Arabic scholars translated Greek and Indian knowledge and added their own. Alcohol. Alkali. Alchemy. Altair. Alkaid. Alcor. Aldebaran. The star names alone tell the story of Arabic astronomy. The chemical terms tell the story of Arabic chemistry. Algorithm tells the story of Arabic mathematics.

NPR noted in 2026: "Other scientific words and names, many beginning with al — Arabic for 'the' — made their way into English during a flowering of Islamic science and math that began in the 8th century. Examples include words like alcohol, alkali, alchemy, and in astronomy, star names such as Altair, Alkaid, Alcor and Aldebaran." Each word is a fossil. A trace of a civilization that preserved and advanced human knowledge while Europe slept. When you say "algorithm," you're speaking Arabic. When you say "alcohol," you're speaking Arabic. When you say "alchemy," you're speaking Arabic. These words are ghosts. They haunt the English language, reminding us that our scientific vocabulary was built on Arabic foundations.

The transition to computing happened slowly. In the 1950s, Edsger Dijkstra developed what became Dijkstra's algorithm — a way of finding the shortest road distance between two points, key to computer-based mapping. Susan McRoy, chair of the Computer Science Department at the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, noted that "long before modern computers, algorithms proved extremely useful in various fields of mathematics, such as business, surveying and navigation." The word algorithm jumped from mathematics to engineering. It was no longer about numbers. It was about paths.

Then Donald Knuth published The Art of Computer Programming in the 1970s, a multi-volume treatise that made algorithms the foundation of computer science. Knuth wrote: "Therefore I wish to propose analysis of algorithms as an appropriate name for the subject matter covered in these books. This name is meant to imply 'the theory of the properties of particular computer algorithms.'" And: "An algorithm must be seen to be believed." And: "The best way to communicate from one human being to another is through story."

Bill Gates said: "If you think you're a really good programmer… read Knuth's Art of Computer Programming… You should definitely send me a résumé if you can read the whole thing." The New York Times called it "the profession's defining treatise." The word had found its home. Algorithm meant computing.

And then the internet happened. And social media. And recommendation engines. And "the algorithm" became the invisible force that decides what you see. The word didn't just describe computation anymore. It described control. A word that meant a step-by-step procedure now meant a step-by-step procedure that decides what you think.

The philosopher G.K. Chesterton once wrote: "There are no uninteresting things, only uninterested people." The algorithm has taken this literally. It watches what you're interested in. It learns your patterns. It adjusts. It gives you more of what you click on, less of what you don't. It creates a world tailored to your preferences. And then it sells that world back to you.

Donald Glover thanked it. Uber drivers cursed it. The Guardian called it "the boss." A word that meant a man from Uzbekistan now means the thing that controls your attention. The thing that decides what you see when you open your phone. The thing that knows you better than you know yourself.

And the man is still there. Underneath the spelling. Underneath the centuries. Underneath the computing and the social media and the recommendation engines. Al-Khwarizmi is still there. His name is still in the word. His voice is still in the sound. His books are still in the libraries. His methods are still in the textbooks. His name is still in your mouth.

He didn't mean to. He just wanted to teach people how to divide numbers. He just wanted to show that Indian numerals were better than Roman ones. He just wanted to solve real problems — inheritance, trade, land measurement. He didn't know that his name would become the word for the thing that controls what seven billion people see every day.

The Library of Congress says he wrote his book "with the encouragement of Caliph Al-Maamoun, the reigning Abbasid caliph of Baghdad in 813--33 AD. It is meant to be a useful work, with examples and applications for everyday life, in areas such as trade, legal inheritance, and surveying."

A useful work. For everyday life. For trade and inheritance and surveying. That's what the book was for. Not for controlling seven billion people's attention. Not for deciding what you see when you open your phone. For solving inheritance disputes. For measuring land. For helping merchants count their goods.

The man from Khwarizma wrote a useful work. And the useful work became the word for the invisible force. The word for the thing that decides what you see. The word for the boss that watches you all the time.
