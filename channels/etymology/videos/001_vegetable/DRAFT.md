# DRAFT — "The Word 'Vegetable'"
## Sleepy Etymology — Episode 1
### Target: 12,600 words | 2 hours at 105 WPM

# SECTION 1: THE HOOK, THE MAP, AND THE COZY OPENING

The year was 1972. A Scottish neurosurgeon named Bryan Jennett and an American neurologist named Fred Plum were sitting in a hospital, staring at patients who presented a problem that had no name. These patients were alive — breathing, their hearts beating, their eyes opening and closing in sleep-wake cycles — but their minds were gone, their awareness absent. The body functioned. The person did not.

They needed a word for this, so they reached back twenty-three hundred years, past modern medicine, past Latin textbooks, to a Greek philosopher who had walked in the Lyceum in Athens in the fourth century before Christ, a man who had divided all of life into three categories. To Aristotle.

They chose his word: vegetative. Persistent vegetative state. A state of being alive without being conscious.

A word that once meant vigorous, active, sprightly, full of life, now meant a person who was awake but absent. A word that once described the power to grow now described the loss of everything that makes us human. How did that happen? How did a word travel from ancient Greek philosophy to a modern hospital room?

For the next two hours, we're going to find out. We're going to follow this word on a journey that spans five thousand years, beginning at the very beginning — with a Proto-Indo-European root that meant to be strong, to be lively — watching it travel through Latin, where it meant to enliven, through Old French, where it meant living, into Middle English, where it meant any plant at all.

We'll sit with Aristotle as he classifies all of life into three tiers. We'll stand in medieval monastery gardens where monks first used the word for food. We'll watch the Enlightenment narrow the word from everything that grows to everything we eat. We'll trace the word's decline — from vigorous to dull to the worst thing that can happen to a human mind.

We'll read passages from Aristotle's On the Soul. We'll read the medical paper that coined persistent vegetative state. We'll read the scientific papers that proved Aristotle wrong — that showed plants can learn, remember, and communicate, that showed the hierarchy the word was built on was a mistake.

We'll follow the word into its own root family — into wake, into vigour, into watch, into witch. All from the same root, all about being alive, all keeping their strength while vegetable lost its own.

And then we'll come back to where we started: to a word that meant alive, and now means broccoli.

Our journey begins five thousand years ago, in a language that was never written down, spoken by people who left no records, no books, no monuments — Proto-Indo-European, the mother of half the languages on earth. In this language, there was a root, *weg-, pronounced roughly wayg, and it meant to be strong, to be lively, to be active. This root is still alive today, living in vigour, in wake, in watch, in vigil, in witch, in Wicca, in wicked — all of these words come from the same place, all of them carrying the original meaning of strength, aliveness, alertness. But one word from this root took a different path, one word traveled from to be strong to broccoli, and that word is vegetable.

Tonight, we follow that word from its roots in a dead language through Aristotle's philosophy, through medieval monastery gardens, through the Enlightenment's love of classification, through modern medicine's need to name the unthinkable, through cutting-edge plant science that proves the ancient philosopher wrong. We follow it because it's a good story, and because the story of a word is the story of how humans think — what we name, what we classify, what we value, and what we forget. So settle in, get comfortable, close your eyes if you like. We have five thousand years to cover, and it begins with a root that meant to be strong.

# SECTION 2: THE ROOT AND THE ROOT FAMILY

The story of vegetable begins with a sound. *weg-. A consonant cluster followed by a short vowel and a hard g. Proto-Indo-European — the reconstructed ancestor language spoken perhaps five thousand years ago, perhaps more, by people who left no written record. We know it only because their words left traces in hundreds of languages that came after.

The root *weg- meant to be strong, to be lively, to be active — vigorously alive, the kind of alive that does things, that moves, that acts. From this root, the Proto-Italic people derived a verb, vegere, meaning to be alive, to be active, to quicken, to make something come alive. From vegere came an adjective, vegetus — a quality, a state of being, something that described vigor and enlivenment. Vegetus meant vigorous, enlivened, active, sprightly. Think of the word vibrant, think of the word alive at its most energetic. That was vegetus.

From vegetus came a verb, vegetare — to enliven, to animate, to give something the quality of vegetus. To vegetate meant to make something alive, to fill it with vitality, the exact opposite of what it means today. From vegetare came an adjective, vegetabilis. In Late Latin, this meant animating, enlivening — something that has the power to enliven, something that gives life. In Medieval Latin, the word shifted slightly, vegetabilis came to mean growing, flourishing, still about the fundamental power of life itself, the power to grow, the power to become.

And then Old French got hold of it. Vegetable in Old French meant living, fit to live. The word traveled from Latin into the Romance languages, carrying its meaning of vitality and growth. Picture the word traveling: from Rome to Gaul, from Latin to Old French. As the Roman Empire collapsed and the languages broke apart, the word broke apart too, losing some of its precision, gaining some of its Romance warmth. Vegetable in Old French was softer than vegetabilis in Latin, less philosophical, more human — living, fit to live, a lived experience rather than an abstraction.

The word spent about five hundred years in Old French, five centuries of spoken language, five centuries of mouths shaping it. From the fall of Rome to the Norman Conquest. And then, in 1066, the Normans conquered England, brought their French with them, and the word came too.

And then, in the early fifteenth century, it entered English. Vegetable — first as an adjective, around 1400. The dictionary says it meant capable of life or growth, growing, vigorous, of material substances, of the plant kingdom, living and growing as a plant. The word described a quality of existence, a category on a grocery shelf.

By the mid-fifteenth century, vegetable had become a noun. It meant non-animal life, any plant — the ones you eat and the ones you don't. Trees were vegetables. Flowers were vegetables. The oak in the forest was a vegetable. The rose in the garden was a vegetable.

The Old English word it replaced was wyrt. We still have this word in St. John's wort and liverwort. Wyrt meant root, herb, vegetable, plant, spice — a broad word for anything that grew. Vegetable displaced it, the Latin-origin word won, and the same PIE root that gave us vegetable also gave us vigour, vigil, wake, watch, witch, Wicca, wicked, velocity, surveillance, reveille, and bivouac — all from *weg-, all from to be strong, be lively.

Vegetable is the one word from this family that lost its power. It went from meaning the power to be alive to meaning the thing you push around your plate, from to be strong to broccoli. The other words kept their strength — vigour is still vigorous, wake is still awake, watch is still watching, vigil is still alert. Even witch and wicked, which took dark turns, kept their energy. A witch is still powerful, wicked still means intense. But vegetable went from strong to edible to dull to brain-dead. It's the word in the family that lost its spine.

Let's spend a moment with each of the major cognates, because they tell us what the root originally meant, before vegetable forgot. Vigour, from Latin vigor, meaning liveliness, activity, force — we still use this word to mean energy, vitality, a person of great vigour is a person of great energy, and the word has not forgotten its roots. Wake, from Old English wacan, to become awake, to arise, to be born, to originate — and from Old English wacian, to be or remain awake. The same root, the same meaning. To be awake is to be alive. To be alive is to be strong.

Watch, from Old English wæccan, meaning to keep watch, to be awake — essentially the same word as wake. A watcher is someone who stays awake, who remains alert, who does not sleep when others do. In medieval cities, there were actual night watchmen, men who walked the streets after dark, carrying lanterns, calling out the hours, watching for fires, for criminals, for trouble. They were the city's eyes through the night. Their job was to stay awake while everyone else slept — to watch, to be the city's vigilance. The word watch comes from their job. To watch is to be awake when others are not, to remain alert when the world is dark.

Vigil, from Latin vigilia, meaning a watch, watchfulness — the state of being awake when you should be sleeping. An Irish wake was a vigil for the dead: the body lay in the house, the family sat with it through the night, they stayed awake, they told stories, they kept the dead company. A wake was a watching, an act of staying alive in the presence of death. From the root that means to be strong, to be lively. The word wake carries both meanings — to wake up, and to keep watch with the dead. The same word, the same root. To be alive and to sit with death.

Witch, from Old English wicce, means a female magician or sorceress — the etymologist Watkins says the word represents a Proto-Germanic *wikkjaz, a necromancer, one who wakes the dead. A witch is someone who has power over life and death, someone who can make the dead rise, from the root that means to be strong, to be lively. In the Laws of Ælfred, written around 890, witchcraft was singled out as a woman's craft, and the Old English text says: Ða fæmnan þe gewuniað onfon gealdorcræftigan & scinlæcan & wiccan, ne læt þu ða libban. The women who are accustomed to receive enchanters and sorceresses and witches, do not let them live. The word wicca renders Latin augur in Anglo-Saxon glossaries, and wicce stands for pythoness, divinatricem — a woman who speaks for the gods, a woman who sees the future. In the Three Kings of Cologne, from around 1400, wicca translates Magi — the wise men, the ones who knew.

Wicca, the modern pagan religion, was named from the Old English word for witch, from the root that means to be strong, to be alive. Wicked, from the same root, originally meant witch-like, then evil, then in modern slang, excellent and intense — a wicked guitar solo, a wicked sunset. The word went from dark to powerful, keeping its energy. Surveillance, French, from sur- over plus veiller to watch, from Latin vigilare, from vigil — watchful, to watch over someone, to keep them under observation. Velocity, from Latin velox, meaning fast, lively — speed that comes from being alive. Reveille, French, the morning call, the signal to wake up, from réveil, awakening. Bivouac, from Swiss German, a temporary military camp, soldiers who stay awake through the night.

All of these words come from the same place, all of them carrying the original meaning of *weg- — strength, aliveness, alertness. And then there's vegetable, the word that went from to be strong to the thing you hide in your mashed potatoes.

How did one word from this family lose everything its siblings kept, how did vegetable forget what it meant to be vigorous while vigour remembered, how did it forget what it meant to be awake while wake remembered? The answer is that the word traveled through too many hands, too many languages, too many centuries, too many people who needed it to mean different things. Each person who used the word gave it a new shade, and over five thousand years the shades accumulated, until the word meant something its original speakers would not recognize.

# SECTION 3: THE WORD ENTERS ENGLISH, THE NARROWING, AND THE DECLINE

Vegetable entered English in the early fifteenth century, around 1400, having traveled from Old French, and before that from Medieval Latin, and before that from Late Latin, and before that from the Proto-Italic verb that meant to be alive. When it arrived in English, it meant something broad, something expansive, something alive — vegetable, as an adjective, meant capable of life or growth, growing, vigorous, living and growing as a plant. And as a noun, vegetable meant any plant, the ones you eat and the ones you don't. The word replaced the Old English wyrt, which had been the common word for anything that grew — root, herb, plant, spice. Wyrt was simple, Wyrt was clear, Wyrt meant anything green. Vegetable displaced it, the Latin-origin word won, and for a few centuries the word meant what it said: any living plant, the whole kingdom, everything that grew from the soil.

And the word was so broad, so expansive, so alive, that it included things that weren't quite plants. In the Middle Ages, a legend spread across Europe about the Vegetable Lamb of Tartary — also called the Scythian Lamb, also called the Barometz. Henry Lee, writing in 1887, described it: Amongst the curious myths of the Middle Ages none were more extravagant and persistent than that of the "Vegetable Lamb of Tartary." This "lamb" was described as being at the same time both a true animal and a living plant.

The story went like this: in Tartary — what is now Russia and Mongolia — there grew a plant that produced gourds, and from these gourds, when they were fully ripe, emerged tiny lambs, perfect in form, in every way resembling an ordinary lamb naturally born, but connected to the plant by an umbilical cord. The lamb grazed on the land around the plant, and when the grass was gone, both lamb and plant died. The fable, Lee wrote, met with almost universal credence from the thirteenth to the seventeenth centuries.

The word vegetable meant "living thing," so a "vegetable lamb" was a living lamb that was also a plant. The boundary between animal and vegetable was blurry, people weren't sure where one ended and the other began, and the word was so broad it could hold both. Sir John Mandeville, the great medieval traveler, wrote about the Vegetable Lamb in his Travels, published around 1357, describing it as growing in Tartary — a plant that bore fruit in the form of a lamb, which people ate. The truth, of course, was cotton: Europeans had heard about cotton from travelers to the East, knew it was a soft white fluffy material that came from a plant, but they had never seen a cotton plant and didn't know how it grew. So they imagined lambs.

Lee argued that the myth originated in the first introduction of cotton and the fabrics woven from it into eastern Europe. The cotton pod, when ripe, bursts open and reveals white fibers — to someone who had never seen cotton, those fibers looked like wool, and wool came from sheep, so the plant must grow sheep. The Vegetable Lamb of Tartary is a story about the word vegetable — a word so broad it could include a lamb, so alive it could describe something that was both animal and plant, a word that didn't yet know its boundaries. Those boundaries were coming.

The Romans had a different word for the plants they ate: olera, from the Latin olus, meaning vegetable. Pliny the Elder wrote about olera, Cato the Elder wrote about them, and in every Roman garden there was a space set apart for olera — the vegetable patch, the place where you grew the things you ate. The Romans didn't use vegetabilis for food, they used it for philosophy: vegetabilis described the quality of being alive, of growing, of flourishing, a word about existence rather than dinner.

The Romans grew cabbage, lettuce, onions, garlic, lentils, beans, cucumbers, and asparagus, and they ate them, cultivated them, wrote about them — but they called them olera, because vegetables was something else, vegetables was about being alive. Cato the Elder, writing in the second century BCE, listed the crops of a Roman farm in order of importance — first the vineyard, then the vegetable garden, then the olive grove — and notice where the vegetable garden sits: second, before the olives, before the grain. The Romans took their olera seriously.

Every Roman garden, no matter how small, had a space set apart for olera — the hortus, behind the house, through the peristyle, past the fountain, a patch of earth where things grew, herbs for seasoning and medicine, vegetables for the table. The garden was the heart of a Roman home, and at the heart of the garden was the olera patch. Pliny the Elder, writing in the first century CE, described the plants that grew in Roman gardens — he cataloged them, classified them, wrote about their properties — but he called them by their names: cabbage, lettuce, beet, turnip. The word vegetable was elsewhere, being philosophical, being Aristotelian, being about the soul.

Then the Roman Empire fell, and the word went underground.

In the medieval period, vegetabilis survived in the monasteries, where monks kept the Latin alive and used it in their texts and to classify. The medieval worldview was hierarchical: everything had its place, God at the top, angels below, humans below angels, animals below humans, plants below animals, rocks at the bottom. The monks used vegetabilis to describe the plant level of this hierarchy — the vegetative level, the level of growth and nutrition but not sensation, not thought. Plants grew, animals felt, humans thought. This was Aristotle's classification, and the monks adopted it.

But the monks also grew things. Every monastery had a garden — the hortus, the place where things grew. The word hortus gives us horticulture, that sense of careful, deliberate growing. And it wasn't just monasteries: Charlemagne, in the late eighth century, issued the Capitulare de villis, his instructions for managing his estates, in which he listed nearly a hundred different herbs, vegetables, nuts, and fruits that should grow in a proper garden. Every estate had to have them, every steward was responsible, and the garden was the heart of the Carolingian economy.

Albertus Magnus — the Dominican friar who was Thomas Aquinas's teacher — wrote a book called De Vegetabilibus et Plantis. On Vegetables and Plants. He compiled a vast amount of earlier knowledge about plants, was one of the first Europeans to study botany systematically, and he used the word vegetabilis, from the root that meant to be alive, to grow, to flourish.

They grew herbs for medicine — lavender for calm, chamomile for sleep, rosemary for memory, thyme for courage. They grew herbs for cooking, basil and parsley and sage. They grew flowers for the altar, roses for the Virgin Mary, lilies for purity. And they used the word vegetable to describe what they grew — a practical label, the plants in the garden, the ones you tend, the ones you harvest, the ones you eat.

Picture a monastery garden: a walled space, enclosed for protection and for prayer, where the monks walked through it in silence in the early morning before the first prayer, touching the plants, checking the soil, pulling weeds. And they called what they grew vegetables, because they were eating them.

This is the moment the word shifts: from philosophy to food, from the soul to the table, from the lowest level of consciousness to the thing you have for supper. The monks didn't notice they were doing it — they were just growing food, but the word was changing under their hands.

By the fifteenth century, vegetable in English meant any plant. But the narrowing had begun, and the word was starting to mean something smaller. The sixteenth century brought the Renaissance and a renewed interest in classification. Naturalists began to categorize the plant world, dividing plants into trees, shrubs, herbs, and gradually vegetables — plants grown for food. The word was no longer about being alive, it was about being useful. The seventeenth century brought the Scientific Revolution and a new way of thinking about plants: they were no longer souls but machines, biological organisms that could be studied, measured, classified. The word vegetable was now firmly about food.

In 1623, Henry Cockeram published his English Dictionarie — one of the first English dictionaries, small, practical, meant to help people use big words. He included the word vegetive, and his definition was: Which liveth as plants do. That was 1623, and the word still meant alive, still meant the quality of being a plant. But look at the phrasing: Liveth as plants do. Not as animals do, not as humans do, as plants do. The hierarchy was already embedded in the definition, living but not like us.

Think about Cockeram for a moment: a man sitting in London, in 1623, writing a dictionary. The Great Plague would hit three years later, the Globe Theatre would burn down the year after that, Shakespeare had died seven years before. And Cockeram was defining words, writing down what they meant, freezing them on the page. His dictionary captured the word vegetable at a moment of transition — still meaning alive, but already carrying the judgment, living as plants do, not as humans do. The word was still broad enough to mean any plant, but the narrowing was coming.

By 1767, the Oxford English Dictionary records the specific sense of vegetable: plant cultivated for culinary purposes, edible herb or root. This is the moment the word narrows. Before 1767, a tree was a vegetable. After 1767, a tree was not a vegetable. A carrot was.

Think about what was lost: the word that once described the power to grow now described only the things we eat, the word that once meant the whole kingdom of plants now meant a subset, a useful subset, a subset arranged on a plate. The eighteenth century brought the Enlightenment and the great classification mania. Carl Linnaeus classified plants by their reproductive organs, dividing them into groups, giving them Latin names, building a system so elegant that we still use it today. Every living thing has a two-part name — Homo sapiens, Rosa canina, Brassica oleracea. That last one, oleracea, comes from olus, the Latin word for vegetable. Even Linnaeus's system remembers the word's history.

Antoine Laurent de Jussieu classified plants by their natural relationships, grouping them by shared features, building a tree of plant life. And in his tree, vegetables were a category — a label in a taxonomy, a branch on a chart. The word was a category in a system, a thing to be classified, measured, studied, and filed.

The nineteenth century brought the Industrial Revolution and the separation of country and city. People stopped growing their own food, they bought vegetables at the market, and the word became a commodity — something you purchased, something you consumed, something with a price. Think about what happened: for centuries, people had grown their own vegetables, touched the soil, watched things grow, pulled carrots from the earth and eaten them. They knew what vegetables were, they were alive, they grew, they were part of the cycle. And then, in a few generations, the connection broke: people moved to cities, lived in apartments, bought food in shops, never saw the soil, never watched things grow. Vegetables became products, things in plastic bags, things with price tags, and the word lost its connection to the living world.

But before that, vegetables had a reputation problem. People feared them. The tomato is the best example: when tomatoes arrived in Europe from the Americas in the sixteenth century, they were met with suspicion. In 1544, the Italian herbalist Pietro Andrea Mattioli classified the tomato as a nightshade — related to deadly belladonna, the plant used to dilate pupils and induce visions. In 1597, the English herbalist John Gerard called tomatoes corrupt and of rank and stinking savor in his book Herbal, essentially sealing the tomato's fate in Great Britain and later the American colonies.

By the eighteenth century, the tomato was nicknamed the poison apple, and aristocrats would fall ill or die after eating them. But it wasn't the tomato that killed them — it was the plates. Aristocrats ate off pewter plates, which contained high levels of lead. Tomatoes are acidic, and the acid leached lead from the plates, giving the aristocrats lead poisoning. They blamed the tomato. Poor people ate off wooden plates and were fine, eating tomatoes with no ill effects. But nobody listened to the poor, and the tomato was guilty.

The fear lasted two hundred years, and the tomato's botanical name still carries the stigma: Lycopersicon esculentum, wolf peach, a name that harkens back to its association with witchcraft, werewolves, and poison. Even the scientific name remembers the fear. And this fear attached itself to the word vegetable: vegetables were plants, plants were suspicious, some plants were poisonous, and the word carried all of this, the suspicion, the fear, the sense that vegetables were not quite trustworthy.

And then something strange happened. In 1854, the word vegetable began to mean dull, uneventful, a vegetable existence, a vegetable life. The word that once meant alive, vigorous, active, sprightly now meant the opposite: stagnant, passive, empty. How did this happen? The word had already narrowed from all plants to food plants, and now it narrowed further, from food plants to the quality of being dull, because the Enlightenment had taught us that plants were simple, passive, unthinking, and the word vegetable carried that judgment.

But before the word went dull, vegetables had a moment of glamour. In 1654, Nicolas de Bonnefons — chamber valet to Louis XIV — published Les Délices de la campagne, one of the first books to treat vegetable cookery as refined cuisine. His philosophy was simple and radical: a cabbage soup should taste entirely of cabbage, a pea soup entirely of peas. Let the vegetable speak. The Coquinist, reviewing the book, called it a foundational text in the European tradition of vegetable-focused cuisine. Bonnefons insisted that each vegetable had its own flavor, its own character, its own dignity — a pea was not just an ingredient, it was a subject.

And peas had already arrived in France with style: when Catherine de Medici married Henri II in 1533, she brought Italian food customs with her, including the fashion for eating peas fresh and green. Before Catherine, peas were dried, stored, eaten in winter. She introduced the idea of eating them young, fresh, sweet, and the fashion spread through the French court. The pea went from peasant food to aristocratic delicacy.

For a moment, vegetables were fashionable, refined, worthy of a king's table. But the moment passed. The Enlightenment came, classification came, and the word vegetable went from meaning alive to meaning food to meaning dull. To be vegetable was to be plant-like, and to be plant-like was to be without thought, without feeling, without consciousness. The hierarchy Aristotle built was still there, still shaping how we used words, still telling us that plants were less than animals, and animals were less than humans.

The twentieth century finished the job. In 1921, vegetable meant person who leads a monotonous life, a couch potato, a vegetable. The word that once described the power to grow now described someone who couldn't move. And then, in 1972, two doctors needed a word for a patient who was alive but not conscious, and they chose vegetative — persistent vegetative state, a state of biological life without mental activity.

And in 1976, vegetable took its final meaning: one totally incapacitated mentally and physically. The word had completed its journey, from alive to food to dull to brain-dead. The Old English word wyrt meant root, herb, vegetable, plant, spice — a word for anything that grew. Vegetable killed it, vegetable displaced it, and then vegetable lost its own meaning. The word that killed wyrt became what wyrt used to describe, and less than that, because wyrt at least meant something alive. Vegetable came to mean something that was barely alive at all.

# SECTION 4: ARISTOTLE AND THE MEDIEVAL WORD

To understand why the word changed, we need to understand the man who made it change: Aristotle, 384 to 322 BCE, born in Macedonia, taught in Athens, the philosopher who classified everything. But before he was a philosopher, Aristotle was a biologist. He spent years on the island of Lesbos, studying marine life. He dissected squid, cataloged fish, watched how octopuses moved. He was fascinated by the difference between things that were alive and things that were not. What makes a fish a fish? What makes a plant a plant? What makes a human a human?

He founded a school in Athens called the Lyceum, where the students walked in a covered walkway called the peripatos — the walkers. Aristotle taught while walking, talked while moving, his philosophy restless, curious, always probing. He was also the tutor of Alexander the Great. Imagine that: the boy who would conquer the known world, sitting at the feet of the man who would classify it. Alexander reportedly sent specimens back to Aristotle from his campaigns, plants and animals from the edges of the known world, the philosopher's curiosity fed by the conqueror's empire.

Aristotle wrote many books, but one of them changed how Western civilization thought about life itself. It was called De Anima — On the Soul — written around 350 BCE, one of the most influential books in Western history. In it, Aristotle asked a simple question: what makes something alive?

His answer was the soul — Aristotle's soul was a set of capacities, a way of asking what can this thing do, what can it become, what makes it alive rather than dead. Aristotle wrote: Now given that there are bodies of such and such a kind, viz. having life, the soul cannot be a body; for the body is the subject or matter, not what is attributed to it. Hence the soul must a substance in the sense of the form of a natural body having life potentially within it.

The soul, Aristotle says, is like the sharpness of an axe — the quality that makes the axe an axe. Cut away the sharpness and you have metal but not an axe. Cut away the soul and you have a body but not a living thing. He uses another analogy: the eye. What is an eye? It's a thing that sees. If an eye were made of stone and couldn't see, it would be an eye in name only, having the shape of an eye but having lost its soul.

And then he goes further: what about appetite? Appetite is the soul's desire for food, and plants have it, animals have it, humans have it — it's the most basic desire, the desire to continue existing, to take in matter and make it part of yourself, to grow, to persist. Aristotle says that appetite is common to all living things, the vegetative soul at work, the most fundamental level of existence, the desire to be alive.

This is strange and beautiful: appetite — hunger, desire, the need to eat — is a vegetative thing, coming from the lowest level of the soul, the level that plants share with us. When you feel hunger, you're feeling what a tree feels when it reaches toward the sun. The same desire, the same drive, the same root. The soul isn't hiding somewhere inside you, waiting to float away when you die. The soul is what you are, the organization of your body, the pattern that makes you alive rather than dead. When the pattern breaks, the soul is gone — it no longer exists.

Aristotle takes it for granted that to have a soul is to be alive, and by life he means self-nutrition and growth and decay — the things that living things do, growing, feeding, reproducing, dying. If a thing does these things, it has a soul. Even plants. So even plants have a kind of soul — a psuchē, the Greek word we get psyche from. But humans have a more complex soul, and this is where Aristotle builds his hierarchy. Broadly speaking, there are three classes of capacities of living things, and Aristotle arranges these hierarchically: higher-order living things possess all the capacities of lower-level living things.

First, nutrition and generation: plants and animals both have this, they can take in food, they can grow, they can make more of themselves. This is the vegetative soul, the nutritive soul, the lowest level. Second, perception: all animals have this, they can see, hear, smell, taste, touch, they can move toward food and away from danger, they can feel pleasure and pain. Animals have everything plants have, plus perception — the sensitive soul. Third, thinking, understanding, reason: humans have this, we can think, we can abstract, we can contemplate the eternal. We have everything animals have, plus reason — the rational soul.

This is Aristotle's great chain of being: plants at the bottom, animals in the middle, humans at the top, and above humans, if you wanted, the divine. The key insight was this: each higher level contained all the levels below it. Humans didn't just have reason — we also had perception, and we also had nutrition and growth. We were everything a plant was, plus everything an animal was, plus reason. The hierarchy was additive. The soul of an apple tree brings apple tree capacities, the soul of a dog dog capacities, the soul of a human human capacities. The exercise of these capacities makes us fully alive and fully who we are.

This framework dominated Western thought for two thousand years, through the Roman period, through the early Christian period, through the medieval period. Thomas Aquinas, the greatest of the medieval theologians, adopted Aristotle's classification wholesale. Albertus Magnus, the teacher of Aquinas, did the same.

Here's how it happened: when the Roman Empire fell, Aristotle's works were lost to Western Europe. They survived in the Arab world, where Islamic scholars — Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd — translated Aristotle into Arabic, commented on him, debated him, and kept his ideas alive while Europe forgot them. Then, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Aristotle's works were translated from Arabic into Latin, arriving in Europe like a flood. Suddenly, the greatest philosopher of antiquity was available again, and the medieval theologians had to reckon with him.

Albertus Magnus, a German Dominican friar, took on the task, writing commentaries on Aristotle and trying to reconcile Aristotle's pagan philosophy with Christian theology. He classified plants and animals using Aristotle's system, and he was one of the first Europeans to study botany systematically. His student, Thomas Aquinas, went further: he built an entire theological system on Aristotle's foundations, arguing that faith and reason were not enemies, that Aristotle's logic could serve Christianity, that the pagan philosopher and the Christian saint were walking the same road.

And in this synthesis, the vegetative soul took on new meaning — it was a theological category, the soul that God gave to plants, the soul that managed the involuntary functions, the soul that kept the body alive without thought or feeling. To be vegetative was to have what God gave to plants, the hierarchy now divine, built into the fabric of creation. To be vegetable was to be at the bottom of God's plan.

This is the weight the word carried when it entered English — a divine hierarchy, a cosmic order. The vegetative was lower, the vegetative was less, the vegetative was not enough. The vegetative soul became a standard category in medieval philosophy and theology — the soul that made the body alive, the soul that managed the involuntary functions: growth, nutrition, reproduction, the things your body does without thinking about them, the things plants do. The medievals used the word vegetativus, from Medieval Latin vegetativus, from vegetat-, the past participle stem of vegetare — the power to enliven, the power to make alive.

But here's where it gets interesting: the medievals also used the word to describe a hierarchy of consciousness. Plants had the vegetative soul, alive but not conscious. Animals had the sensitive soul, conscious but not rational. Humans had the rational soul, conscious and rational. This hierarchy was moral, spiritual, the vegetative was lower, the rational was higher. To be vegetable was to be less, to be human was to be more.

And this is where the word began to change — in value. To be called vegetative was a judgment, a statement that you were at the bottom of the hierarchy, alive but barely, growing but not feeling, existing but not thinking. The word carried this moral weight for centuries, through the Renaissance, through the Reformation, through the Enlightenment, each era adding its own layer. The Enlightenment valued reason above all else, and to be vegetable was to lack reason, to be less than human.

Cockeram's English Dictionarie, published in 1623, defined vegetive as Which liveth as plants do — the judgment was already there, living as plants do, not as humans do, the hierarchy embedded in the definition. Monasteries were the word's home, where monks kept the Latin alive and used vegetabilis in their texts and to classify, and they used it in their gardens. Every monastery had a garden — the hortus, the place where things grew — and in the hortus the monks grew herbs for medicine, food for the table, flowers for the altar, and they used the word vegetable to describe what they grew. But the monks also inherited Aristotle's hierarchy: they knew that plants were below animals in the chain of being, that the vegetative soul was the lowest level, and this knowledge shaped how they used the word. To call something vegetable was to place it in the hierarchy, to say it was alive but not as alive as an animal, a human, an angel. The word carried the entire weight of medieval cosmology.

By the time the word entered English, in the early fifteenth century, it carried all of this — it meant alive, but it meant alive at the bottom of the hierarchy, alive but less, alive but not enough. The word was waiting for what would happen next: the narrowing, the decline, the moment when alive would become food, and food would become dull, and dull would become brain-dead. Aristotle built the hierarchy, the monks maintained it, the Enlightenment reinforced it, and medicine completed it.

The word vegetative traveled from Aristotle's Lyceum to a modern hospital room, a journey of twenty-three hundred years. And every step of the way, it carried the same judgment: the vegetative is lower, the vegetative is less, the vegetative is not enough. Until the science proved otherwise. But we'll get to that.

For now, let the word settle: vegetable, a word that once meant the power to grow, now means the loss of everything that makes us human. Aristotle's ghost, walking through a hospital corridor, his classification naming the unthinkable.

# SECTION 5: THE MEDICAL HIJACKING, THE MISDIAGNOSIS, AND PLANT INTELLIGENCE

In 1972, Bryan Jennett and Fred Plum published a paper in the Lancet. They needed a name for a condition they were seeing more and more often: patients who were alive, their eyes opening and closing, their bodies breathing, their hearts beating, but whose minds were absent, whose awareness was gone.

They called it the persistent vegetative state.

The word vegetative came, as we've seen, from Aristotle. But it had traveled a long road to reach Jennett and Plum. Along the way, it passed through the hands of two other thinkers: Xavier Bichat, a French anatomist who worked in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, and Walter Timme, an American neurologist in the early twentieth century. Bichat used the phrase vegetative nervous system, meaning the part of the nervous system that managed involuntary functions — breathing, digestion, heartbeat, the things the body does without thinking, the things plants do. Timme continued this usage, speaking of the vegetative functions, the automatic functions, the functions that keep the body alive but don't require consciousness.

And then Jennett and Plum took the word vegetative and applied it to a state of consciousness — or rather, a state of unconsciousness, a state where the body functioned but the mind was gone.

Zoe Adams and Joseph Fins, in a 2016 paper in the Journal of the History of the Neurosciences, traced the etymology. They wrote: The persistent vegetative state is one of the most iconic and misunderstood phrases in clinical neuroscience. Coined as a diagnostic category by Scottish neurosurgeon Bryan Jennett and American neurologist Fred Plum in 1972, the phrase vegetative first appeared in Aristotle's treatise On the Soul, circa mid-fourth century BCE.

A word from 350 BCE, a word about the lowest level of the soul, a word about what plants have that animals and humans also have — applied to patients in 1972, patients who were alive but absent. The word traveled twenty-three hundred years to name the worst thing that can happen to a human mind.

The NEJM consensus statement of 1994 defined the condition: The vegetative state is a clinical condition of complete unawareness of the self and the environment, accompanied by sleep-wake cycles, with either complete or partial preservation of hypothalamic and brain-stem autonomic functions. Patients in a vegetative state show no evidence of sustained, reproducible, purposeful, or voluntary behavioral responses to visual, auditory, tactile, or noxious stimuli. They show no evidence of language comprehension or expression. They have bowel and bladder incontinence. They have variably preserved cranial-nerve and spinal reflexes.

The body is alive, but the person is absent.

The word hides more than it reveals: the word vegetative tells you the body is alive, but it doesn't tell you whether the person is there, whether they can hear you, whether they can feel you, whether they're trapped inside, unable to speak, unable to move, yet aware. The diagnosis is wrong a lot of the time — Andreas Bender and colleagues, in a 2015 paper, found that the rate of misdiagnosis is 37% to 43%, many patients wrongly diagnosed as vegetative when careful evaluation would reveal at least some preserved consciousness. Bender wrote: Contrary to prior assumptions, 10% to 24% of patients in PVS can regain consciousness, sometimes years after the event, but only with marked functional impairment. Ten to twenty-four percent — one in four, or one in ten. Patients who were called vegetative, patients who were written off, who came back.

The word hides more than it reveals: the word vegetative tells you the body is alive, but it doesn't tell you whether the person is there, whether they can hear you, whether they can feel you, whether they're trapped inside, unable to speak, unable to move, yet aware. Some of these patients can, some of them are there, and the word vegetative says they're not.

In 2006, Adrian Owen and colleagues at the University of Cambridge published a study in the journal Science called Detecting awareness in the vegetative state. They had a patient — a woman who had been in a vegetative state for five months after a car accident, whom the doctors said was not conscious, with the word vegetative on her chart. Owen put her in an fMRI machine, a brain scanner, and gave her instructions: imagine you are playing tennis, imagine you are hitting the ball, swinging your arms, moving around the court. Her brain lit up — the motor cortex, the supplementary motor area, the same areas that light up in a healthy person imagining tennis. She was doing it, she was following instructions, she was conscious. Then Owen gave her another instruction: imagine you are walking through the rooms of your house, start at the front door, walk into the living room, walk into the kitchen. Her brain lit up again, different areas this time, the spatial navigation areas, the places in the brain that handle maps and rooms and movement through space. She was imagining her house. She was there.

A woman the doctors said was not conscious, a woman labeled vegetative, following instructions in an fMRI machine, answering questions with her brain because she couldn't answer with her voice. The word vegetative was wrong. She was there, and she had been there all along.

While medicine was using this word to describe the absence of consciousness, science was discovering something that Aristotle never imagined, something that undermines the entire hierarchy the word was built on: plants are intelligent. In the way we usually mean intelligence — something different, something we don't have a word for yet. In 2014, Monica Gagliano, a researcher at the University of Western Australia, published a paper that shook the botanical world, called Experience teaches plants to learn faster and forget slower in environments where it matters, published in the journal Oecologia. Gagliano had been studying Mimosa pudica, the sensitive plant, the one that folds its leaves when you touch it — a simple reflex. But Gagliano asked a question nobody had asked before: could the plant learn?

The experiment was simple. She took the plants and dropped them — not from a great height, just a short fall, enough to trigger the leaf-folding response. The leaves would snap shut, the plant would protect itself, a reflex, an automatic response, no thinking required. She dropped them again and again, drop after drop, fall after fall. At first, the plants folded their leaves every time, responding to every drop with the same reflex, the same protection, over and over. But after a while, something changed: the plants stopped folding their leaves, they habituated, they learned that the drop was harmless, that there was no danger, that they didn't need to respond. They stopped wasting energy on a stimulus that meant nothing.

This is learning — classical conditioning, the same kind of learning Pavlov demonstrated with dogs. Pavlov rang a bell and gave the dogs food, and after a while the dogs salivated at the bell alone, the bell becoming associated with food, the dogs learning. Gagliano dropped the plants and nothing happened, and after a while the plants learned that the drop meant nothing, stopping their response without a brain, without neurons, without anything we'd recognize as a mind. They learned.

And here's the remarkable part: Gagliano left the plants alone for a month, no drops, no disturbance. They sat in their pots, they grew, they did whatever plants do when no one is watching. And then, after a month, Gagliano came back and dropped them again. They didn't fold their leaves. They remembered. For a month, without a brain, they remembered that the drop was harmless. Astonishingly, Gagliano wrote, Mimosa can display the learned response even when left undisturbed in a more favourable environment for a month — a month without a brain, without neurons, without anything we'd recognize as a nervous system, and the plant remembered.

In 2020, Paco Calvo, Monica Gagliano, Gustavo Souza, and Anthony Trewavas published a paper in the Annals of Botany called Plants are intelligent, here's how. They argued that intelligence is a property that an individual plant has as it interacts with its environment — intelligence, the ability to assess a situation and change behavior to improve the outcome. Intelligence, they wrote, is related to the plant's ability to succeed or profit with respect to some goal or objective.

Stefano Mancuso, at the University of Florence, has taken this further. He founded the International Laboratory of Plant Neurobiology, and his research shows that plants communicate through chemical signals — volatile organic compounds — and that they can distinguish kin from non-kin, recognize their neighbors, warn each other about predators. Some scientists think the term plant neurobiology is misleading: plants have no neurons, no brains, and calling their signaling system neurobiology is anthropomorphizing, projecting human qualities onto plants. Mancuso doesn't care. He argues that the term neurobiology is provocative precisely because it forces us to reconsider what we mean by intelligence — if a plant can learn without a brain, what does that tell us about the relationship between brains and intelligence? Maybe intelligence requires something else, something we haven't named yet.

The idea that plants are low-level organisms dates back to Aristotle, Mancuso has said. The great philosopher believed that plants only possess a vegetative soul, as they lack movement and therefore do not need to sense. That they are on the edge between living and not living. Today we know that plants do not simply survive, but are highly complex living organisms with an ability to sense, far more sophisticated in sensing than animals, and able to communicate. Plants are extraordinary communicators: they communicate with other plants, distinguish kin from non-kin, communicate with plants of other species, communicate with animals by producing chemical volatiles during pollination. They can count, make decisions, learn from experience, remember. And they communicate underground, through a network of fungi called mycorrhizae, the fungi connecting the roots of different trees, forming a web, a network. Some scientists call it the wood wide web, and through this web trees share nutrients, send chemical signals, warn each other about insect attacks. A tree under siege by caterpillars can send a chemical signal through the fungal network, and neighboring trees will increase their chemical defenses before the caterpillars even arrive.

Trees share food through the network: a mother tree can send carbon to a shaded seedling, a dying tree can dump its resources into the network for others to use. The forest is a community, a superorganism. Aristotle said plants were on the edge between living and not living. He was wrong. Plants are at the center of a network that connects them to each other and to the soil and to the fungi and to the insects and to the entire ecosystem. They are alive in ways we are only beginning to understand.

And now the word vegetable comes full circle. The word was built on Aristotle's hierarchy — the word vegetative meant the lowest level, the level of growth without thought, of life without consciousness. But the science has demolished the hierarchy. Plants are not less alive, they are alive differently, intelligent in ways we don't fully understand. The word carries a hierarchy that science has proved wrong: every time we say vegetative, we are using a word that assumes plants are simple, passive, less. The science says otherwise. The word remembers what Aristotle forgot: that life is not a hierarchy. It is a network. And plants are part of it.
