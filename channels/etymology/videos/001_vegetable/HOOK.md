# VEGETABLE — HOOK AND OPENING

## The Hook (first 2 minutes)

The year was 1972. A Scottish neurosurgeon named Bryan Jennett and an American neurologist named Fred Plum were sitting in a hospital. They had a problem. They had patients who were alive — breathing, heartbeat, sleep-wake cycles — but who were not there. Not conscious. Not aware. The body functioned. The person was gone.

They needed a word for this.

So they reached back. Not to modern medicine. Not to Latin textbooks. They reached back twenty-three hundred years. To a Greek philosopher who had walked in the Lyceum in Athens. To a man who had divided all of life into three categories. To Aristotle.

They chose his word. Vegetative. Persistent vegetative state. A state of being alive without being conscious.

Think about that. A word that once meant vigorous. Active. Sprightly. Alive. Now meant a person who was awake but not aware. A word that once described the power to grow now described the loss of everything that makes us human.

How did that happen? How did a word travel from ancient Greek philosophy to a modern hospital room? How did it go from meaning alive to meaning not really alive?

For the next two hours, we're going to find out.

## The Map (next 2-3 minutes)

We're going to follow this word on a journey that spans five thousand years. We'll begin at the very beginning — with a Proto-Indo-European root that meant to be strong, to be lively. We'll watch it travel through Latin, where it meant to enliven. Through Old French, where it meant living. Into Middle English, where it meant any plant at all.

We'll sit with Aristotle as he classifies all of life. We'll stand in medieval monastery gardens where monks first used the word for food. We'll watch the Enlightenment narrow the word from everything that grows to everything we eat.

We'll read passages from Aristotle's On the Soul. We'll read the medical paper that coined persistent vegetative state. We'll read the scientific papers that proved Aristotle wrong — that showed plants can learn, remember, communicate.

We'll follow the word into its own root family. Into wake. Into vigour. Into watch. Into witch. All from the same root. All about being alive.

And then we'll come back to where we started. To a word that meant alive. And now means broccoli.

## The Cozy Opening (next 2-3 minutes)

Our journey begins five thousand years ago. In a language that was never written down. Spoken by people who left no records. Proto-Indo-European. The mother of half the languages on earth.

In this language, there was a root. *weg-. Pronounced roughly wayg. It meant to be strong. To be lively. To be active.

This root is still alive today. It lives in vigour. In wake. In watch. In vigil. In witch. In Wicca. In wicked. All of these words come from the same place. All of them carry the original meaning. Strength. Aliveness. Alertness.

But one word from this root took a different path. One word traveled from to be strong to broccoli. And that word is vegetable.

Tonight, we follow that word. We follow it from its roots in a dead language through Aristotle's philosophy, through medieval monastery gardens, through the Enlightenment's love of classification, through modern medicine's need to name the unthinkable, through cutting-edge plant science that proves the ancient philosopher wrong.

We follow it because it's a good story. And because the story of a word is the story of how humans think. What we name. What we classify. What we value. And what we forget.

So settle in. Get comfortable. Close your eyes if you like. We have five thousand years to cover. And it begins with a root that meant to be strong.
