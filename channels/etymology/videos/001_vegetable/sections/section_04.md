
# SECTION 3: THE WORD ENTERS ENGLISH, THE NARROWING, AND THE DECLINE

Vegetable entered English in the early fifteenth century, around 1400, having traveled from Old French, and before that from Medieval Latin, and before that from Late Latin, and before that from the Proto-Italic verb that meant to be alive. When it arrived in English, it meant something broad, something expansive, something alive — vegetable, as an adjective, meant capable of life or growth, growing, vigorous, living and growing as a plant. And as a noun, vegetable meant any plant, the ones you eat and the ones you don't. The word replaced the Old English wyrt, which had been the common word for anything that grew — root, herb, plant, spice. Wyrt was simple, Wyrt was clear, Wyrt meant anything green. Vegetable displaced it, the Latin-origin word won, and for a few centuries the word meant what it said: any living plant, the whole kingdom, everything that grew from the soil.

And the word was so broad, so expansive, so alive, that it included things that weren't quite plants. In the Middle Ages, a legend spread across Europe about the Vegetable Lamb of Tartary — also called the Scythian Lamb, also called the Barometz. Henry Lee, writing in 1887, described it: Amongst the curious myths of the Middle Ages none were more extravagant and persistent than that of the "Vegetable Lamb of Tartary." This "lamb" was described as being at the same time both a true animal and a living plant.

The story went like this: in Tartary — what is now Russia and Mongolia — there grew a plant that produced gourds, and from these gourds, when they were fully ripe, emerged tiny lambs, perfect in form, in every way resembling an ordinary lamb naturally born, but connected to the plant by an umbilical cord. The lamb grazed on the land around the plant, and when the grass was gone, both lamb and plant died. The fable, Lee wrote, met with almost universal credence from the thirteenth to the seventeenth centuries.

The word vegetable meant "living thing," so a "vegetable lamb" was a living lamb that was also a plant. The boundary between animal and vegetable was blurry, people weren't sure where one ended and the other began, and the word was so broad it could hold both. Sir John Mandeville, the great medieval traveler, wrote about the Vegetable Lamb in his Travels, published around 1357, describing it as growing in Tartary — a plant that bore fruit in the form of a lamb, which people ate. The truth, of course, was cotton: Europeans had heard about cotton from travelers to the East, knew it was a soft white fluffy material that came from a plant, but they had never seen a cotton plant and didn't know how it grew. So they imagined lambs.

Lee argued that the myth originated in the first introduction of cotton and the fabrics woven from it into eastern Europe. The cotton pod, when ripe, bursts open and reveals white fibers — to someone who had never seen cotton, those fibers looked like wool, and wool came from sheep, so the plant must grow sheep. The Vegetable Lamb of Tartary is a story about the word vegetable — a word so broad it could include a lamb, so alive it could describe something that was both animal and plant, a word that didn't yet know its boundaries. Those boundaries were coming.

The Romans had a different word for the plants they ate: olera, from the Latin olus, meaning vegetable. Pliny the Elder wrote about olera, Cato the Elder wrote about them, and in every Roman garden there was a space set apart for olera — the vegetable patch, the place where you grew the things you ate. The Romans didn't use vegetabilis for food, they used it for philosophy: vegetabilis described the quality of being alive, of growing, of flourishing, a word about existence rather than dinner.

The Romans grew cabbage, lettuce, onions, garlic, lentils, beans, cucumbers, and asparagus, and they ate them, cultivated them, wrote about them — but they called them olera, because vegetables was something else, vegetables was about being alive. Cato the Elder, writing in the second century BCE, listed the crops of a Roman farm in order of importance — first the vineyard, then the vegetable garden, then the olive grove — and notice where the vegetable garden sits: second, before the olives, before the grain. The Romans took their olera seriously.

Every Roman garden, no matter how small, had a space set apart for olera — the hortus, behind the house, through the peristyle, past the fountain, a patch of earth where things grew, herbs for seasoning and medicine, vegetables for the table. The garden was the heart of a Roman home, and at the heart of the garden was the olera patch. Pliny the Elder, writing in the first century CE, described the plants that grew in Roman gardens — he cataloged them, classified them, wrote about their properties — but he called them by their names: cabbage, lettuce, beet, turnip. The word vegetable was elsewhere, being philosophical, being Aristotelian, being about the soul.

Then the Roman Empire fell, and the word went underground.

In the medieval period, vegetabilis survived in the monasteries, where monks kept the Latin alive and used it in their texts and to classify. The medieval worldview was hierarchical: everything had its place, God at the top, angels below, humans below angels, animals below humans, plants below animals, rocks at the bottom. The monks used vegetabilis to describe the plant level of this hierarchy — the vegetative level, the level of growth and nutrition but not sensation, not thought. Plants grew, animals felt, humans thought. This was Aristotle's classification, and the monks adopted it.

But the monks also grew things. Every monastery had a garden — the hortus, the place where things grew. The word hortus gives us horticulture, that sense of careful, deliberate growing. And it wasn't just monasteries: Charlemagne, in the late eighth century, issued the Capitulare de villis, his instructions for managing his estates, in which he listed nearly a hundred different herbs, vegetables, nuts, and fruits that should grow in a proper garden. Every estate had to have them, every steward was responsible, and the garden was the heart of the Carolingian economy.

Albertus Magnus — the Dominican friar who was Thomas Aquinas's teacher — wrote a book called De Vegetabilibus et Plantis. On Vegetables and Plants. He compiled a vast amount of earlier knowledge about plants, was one of the first Europeans to study botany systematically, and he used the word vegetabilis, from the root that meant to be alive, to grow, to flourish.

They grew herbs for medicine — lavender for calm, chamomile for sleep, rosemary for memory, thyme for courage. They grew herbs for cooking, basil and parsley and sage. They grew flowers for the altar, roses for the Virgin Mary, lilies for purity. And they used the word vegetable to describe what they grew — a practical label, the plants in the garden, the ones you tend, the ones you harvest, the ones you eat.

Picture a monastery garden: a walled space, enclosed for protection and for prayer, where the monks walked through it in silence in the early morning before the first prayer, touching the plants, checking the soil, pulling weeds. And they called what they grew vegetables, because they were eating them.

This is the moment the word shifts: from philosophy to food, from the soul to the table, from the lowest level of consciousness to the thing you have for supper. The monks didn't notice they were doing it — they were just growing food, but the word was changing under their hands.

By the fifteenth century, vegetable in English meant any plant. But the narrowing had begun, and the word was starting to mean something smaller. The sixteenth century brought the Renaissance and a renewed interest in classification. Naturalists began to categorize the plant world, dividing plants into trees, shrubs, herbs, and gradually vegetables — plants grown for food. The word was no longer about being alive, it was about being useful. The seventeenth century brought the Scientific Revolution and a new way of thinking about plants: they were no longer souls but machines, biological organisms that could be studied, measured, classified. The word vegetable was now firmly about food.

In 1623, Henry Cockeram published his English Dictionarie — one of the first English dictionaries, small, practical, meant to help people use big words. He included the word vegetive, and his definition was: Which liveth as plants do. That was 1623, and the word still meant alive, still meant the quality of being a plant. But look at the phrasing: Liveth as plants do. Not as animals do, not as humans do, as plants do. The hierarchy was already embedded in the definition, living but not like us.

Think about Cockeram for a moment: a man sitting in London, in 1623, writing a dictionary. The Great Plague would hit three years later, the Globe Theatre would burn down the year after that, Shakespeare had died seven years before. And Cockeram was defining words, writing down what they meant, freezing them on the page. His dictionary captured the word vegetable at a moment of transition — still meaning alive, but already carrying the judgment, living as plants do, not as humans do. The word was still broad enough to mean any plant, but the narrowing was coming.

By 1767, the Oxford English Dictionary records the specific sense of vegetable: plant cultivated for culinary purposes, edible herb or root. This is the moment the word narrows. Before 1767, a tree was a vegetable. After 1767, a tree was not a vegetable. A carrot was.

Think about what was lost: the word that once described the power to grow now described only the things we eat, the word that once meant the whole kingdom of plants now meant a subset, a useful subset, a subset arranged on a plate. The eighteenth century brought the Enlightenment and the great classification mania. Carl Linnaeus classified plants by their reproductive organs, dividing them into groups, giving them Latin names, building a system so elegant that we still use it today. Every living thing has a two-part name — Homo sapiens, Rosa canina, Brassica oleracea. That last one, oleracea, comes from olus, the Latin word for vegetable. Even Linnaeus's system remembers the word's history.

Antoine Laurent de Jussieu classified plants by their natural relationships, grouping them by shared features, building a tree of plant life. And in his tree, vegetables were a category — a label in a taxonomy, a branch on a chart. The word was a category in a system, a thing to be classified, measured, studied, and filed.

The nineteenth century brought the Industrial Revolution and the separation of country and city. People stopped growing their own food, they bought vegetables at the market, and the word became a commodity — something you purchased, something you consumed, something with a price. Think about what happened: for centuries, people had grown their own vegetables, touched the soil, watched things grow, pulled carrots from the earth and eaten them. They knew what vegetables were, they were alive, they grew, they were part of the cycle. And then, in a few generations, the connection broke: people moved to cities, lived in apartments, bought food in shops, never saw the soil, never watched things grow. Vegetables became products, things in plastic bags, things with price tags, and the word lost its connection to the living world.

But before that, vegetables had a reputation problem. People feared them. The tomato is the best example: when tomatoes arrived in Europe from the Americas in the sixteenth century, they were met with suspicion. In 1544, the Italian herbalist Pietro Andrea Mattioli classified the tomato as a nightshade — related to deadly belladonna, the plant used to dilate pupils and induce visions. In 1597, the English herbalist John Gerard called tomatoes corrupt and of rank and stinking savor in his book Herbal, essentially sealing the tomato's fate in Great Britain and later the American colonies.

By the eighteenth century, the tomato was nicknamed the poison apple, and aristocrats would fall ill or die after eating them. But it wasn't the tomato that killed them — it was the plates. Aristocrats ate off pewter plates, which contained high levels of lead. Tomatoes are acidic, and the acid leached lead from the plates, giving the aristocrats lead poisoning. They blamed the tomato. Poor people ate off wooden plates and were fine, eating tomatoes with no ill effects. But nobody listened to the poor, and the tomato was guilty.

The fear lasted two hundred years, and the tomato's botanical name still carries the stigma: Lycopersicon esculentum, wolf peach, a name that harkens back to its association with witchcraft, werewolves, and poison. Even the scientific name remembers the fear. And this fear attached itself to the word vegetable: vegetables were plants, plants were suspicious, some plants were poisonous, and the word carried all of this, the suspicion, the fear, the sense that vegetables were not quite trustworthy.

And then something strange happened. In 1854, the word vegetable began to mean dull, uneventful, a vegetable existence, a vegetable life. The word that once meant alive, vigorous, active, sprightly now meant the opposite: stagnant, passive, empty. How did this happen? The word had already narrowed from all plants to food plants, and now it narrowed further, from food plants to the quality of being dull, because the Enlightenment had taught us that plants were simple, passive, unthinking, and the word vegetable carried that judgment.

But before the word went dull, vegetables had a moment of glamour. In 1654, Nicolas de Bonnefons — chamber valet to Louis XIV — published Les Délices de la campagne, one of the first books to treat vegetable cookery as refined cuisine. His philosophy was simple and radical: a cabbage soup should taste entirely of cabbage, a pea soup entirely of peas. Let the vegetable speak. The Coquinist, reviewing the book, called it a foundational text in the European tradition of vegetable-focused cuisine. Bonnefons insisted that each vegetable had its own flavor, its own character, its own dignity — a pea was not just an ingredient, it was a subject.

And peas had already arrived in France with style: when Catherine de Medici married Henri II in 1533, she brought Italian food customs with her, including the fashion for eating peas fresh and green. Before Catherine, peas were dried, stored, eaten in winter. She introduced the idea of eating them young, fresh, sweet, and the fashion spread through the French court. The pea went from peasant food to aristocratic delicacy.

For a moment, vegetables were fashionable, refined, worthy of a king's table. But the moment passed. The Enlightenment came, classification came, and the word vegetable went from meaning alive to meaning food to meaning dull. To be vegetable was to be plant-like, and to be plant-like was to be without thought, without feeling, without consciousness. The hierarchy Aristotle built was still there, still shaping how we used words, still telling us that plants were less than animals, and animals were less than humans.

The twentieth century finished the job. In 1921, vegetable meant person who leads a monotonous life, a couch potato, a vegetable. The word that once described the power to grow now described someone who couldn't move. And then, in 1972, two doctors needed a word for a patient who was alive but not conscious, and they chose vegetative — persistent vegetative state, a state of biological life without mental activity.

And in 1976, vegetable took its final meaning: one totally incapacitated mentally and physically. The word had completed its journey, from alive to food to dull to brain-dead. The Old English word wyrt meant root, herb, vegetable, plant, spice — a word for anything that grew. Vegetable killed it, vegetable displaced it, and then vegetable lost its own meaning. The word that killed wyrt became what wyrt used to describe, and less than that, because wyrt at least meant something alive. Vegetable came to mean something that was barely alive at all.
