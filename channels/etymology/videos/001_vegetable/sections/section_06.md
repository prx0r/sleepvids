
# SECTION 5: THE MEDICAL HIJACKING, THE MISDIAGNOSIS, AND PLANT INTELLIGENCE

In 1972, Bryan Jennett and Fred Plum published a paper in the Lancet. They needed a name for a condition they were seeing more and more often: patients who were alive, their eyes opening and closing, their bodies breathing, their hearts beating, but whose minds were absent, whose awareness was gone.

They called it the persistent vegetative state.

The word vegetative came, as we've seen, from Aristotle. But it had traveled a long road to reach Jennett and Plum. Along the way, it passed through the hands of two other thinkers: Xavier Bichat, a French anatomist who worked in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, and Walter Timme, an American neurologist in the early twentieth century. Bichat used the phrase vegetative nervous system, meaning the part of the nervous system that managed involuntary functions — breathing, digestion, heartbeat, the things the body does without thinking, the things plants do. Timme continued this usage, speaking of the vegetative functions, the automatic functions, the functions that keep the body alive but don't require consciousness.

And then Jennett and Plum took the word vegetative and applied it to a state of consciousness — or rather, a state of unconsciousness, a state where the body functioned but the mind was gone.

Zoe Adams and Joseph Fins, in a 2016 paper in the Journal of the History of the Neurosciences, traced the etymology. They wrote: The persistent vegetative state is one of the most iconic and misunderstood phrases in clinical neuroscience. Coined as a diagnostic category by Scottish neurosurgeon Bryan Jennett and American neurologist Fred Plum in 1972, the phrase vegetative first appeared in Aristotle's treatise On the Soul, circa mid-fourth century BCE.

A word from 350 BCE, a word about the lowest level of the soul, a word about what plants have that animals and humans also have — applied to patients in 1972, patients who were alive but absent. The word traveled twenty-three hundred years to name the worst thing that can happen to a human mind.

The NEJM consensus statement of 1994 defined the condition: The vegetative state is a clinical condition of complete unawareness of the self and the environment, accompanied by sleep-wake cycles, with either complete or partial preservation of hypothalamic and brain-stem autonomic functions. Patients in a vegetative state show no evidence of sustained, reproducible, purposeful, or voluntary behavioral responses to visual, auditory, tactile, or noxious stimuli. They show no evidence of language comprehension or expression. They have bowel and bladder incontinence. They have variably preserved cranial-nerve and spinal reflexes.

The body is alive, but the person is absent.

The word hides more than it reveals: the word vegetative tells you the body is alive, but it doesn't tell you whether the person is there, whether they can hear you, whether they can feel you, whether they're trapped inside, unable to speak, unable to move, yet aware. The diagnosis is wrong a lot of the time — Andreas Bender and colleagues, in a 2015 paper, found that the rate of misdiagnosis is 37% to 43%, many patients wrongly diagnosed as vegetative when careful evaluation would reveal at least some preserved consciousness. Bender wrote: Contrary to prior assumptions, 10% to 24% of patients in PVS can regain consciousness, sometimes years after the event, but only with marked functional impairment. Ten to twenty-four percent — one in four, or one in ten. Patients who were called vegetative, patients who were written off, who came back.

Some of these patients can, some of them are there, and the word vegetative says they're not.

In 2006, Adrian Owen and colleagues at the University of Cambridge published a study in the journal Science called Detecting awareness in the vegetative state. They had a patient — a woman who had been in a vegetative state for five months after a car accident, whom the doctors said was not conscious, with the word vegetative on her chart. Owen put her in an fMRI machine, a brain scanner, and gave her instructions: imagine you are playing tennis, imagine you are hitting the ball, swinging your arms, moving around the court. Her brain lit up — the motor cortex, the supplementary motor area, the same areas that light up in a healthy person imagining tennis. She was doing it, she was following instructions, she was conscious. Then Owen gave her another instruction: imagine you are walking through the rooms of your house, start at the front door, walk into the living room, walk into the kitchen. Her brain lit up again, different areas this time, the spatial navigation areas, the places in the brain that handle maps and rooms and movement through space. She was imagining her house. She was there.

A woman the doctors said was not conscious, a woman labeled vegetative, following instructions in an fMRI machine, answering questions with her brain because she couldn't answer with her voice. The word vegetative was wrong. She was there, and she had been there all along.

While medicine was using this word to describe the absence of consciousness, science was discovering something that Aristotle never imagined, something that undermines the entire hierarchy the word was built on: plants are intelligent. In the way we usually mean intelligence — something different, something we don't have a word for yet. In 2014, Monica Gagliano, a researcher at the University of Western Australia, published a paper that shook the botanical world, called Experience teaches plants to learn faster and forget slower in environments where it matters, published in the journal Oecologia. Gagliano had been studying Mimosa pudica, the sensitive plant, the one that folds its leaves when you touch it — a simple reflex. But Gagliano asked a question nobody had asked before: could the plant learn?

The experiment was simple. She took the plants and dropped them — not from a great height, just a short fall, enough to trigger the leaf-folding response. The leaves would snap shut, the plant would protect itself, a reflex, an automatic response, no thinking required. She dropped them again and again, drop after drop, fall after fall. At first, the plants folded their leaves every time, responding to every drop with the same reflex, the same protection, over and over. But after a while, something changed: the plants stopped folding their leaves, they habituated, they learned that the drop was harmless, that there was no danger, that they didn't need to respond. They stopped wasting energy on a stimulus that meant nothing.

This is learning — classical conditioning, the same kind of learning Pavlov demonstrated with dogs. Pavlov rang a bell and gave the dogs food, and after a while the dogs salivated at the bell alone, the bell becoming associated with food, the dogs learning. Gagliano dropped the plants and nothing happened, and after a while the plants learned that the drop meant nothing, stopping their response without a brain, without neurons, without anything we'd recognize as a mind. They learned.

And here's the remarkable part: Gagliano left the plants alone for a month, no drops, no disturbance. They sat in their pots, they grew, they did whatever plants do when no one is watching. And then, after a month, Gagliano came back and dropped them again. They didn't fold their leaves. They remembered. For a month, without a brain, they remembered that the drop was harmless. Astonishingly, Gagliano wrote, Mimosa can display the learned response even when left undisturbed in a more favourable environment for a month — a month without a brain, without neurons, without anything we'd recognize as a nervous system, and the plant remembered.

In 2020, Paco Calvo, Monica Gagliano, Gustavo Souza, and Anthony Trewavas published a paper in the Annals of Botany called Plants are intelligent, here's how. They argued that intelligence is a property that an individual plant has as it interacts with its environment — intelligence, the ability to assess a situation and change behavior to improve the outcome. Intelligence, they wrote, is related to the plant's ability to succeed or profit with respect to some goal or objective.

Stefano Mancuso, at the University of Florence, has taken this further. He founded the International Laboratory of Plant Neurobiology, and his research shows that plants communicate through chemical signals — volatile organic compounds — and that they can distinguish kin from non-kin, recognize their neighbors, warn each other about predators. Some scientists think the term plant neurobiology is misleading: plants have no neurons, no brains, and calling their signaling system neurobiology is anthropomorphizing, projecting human qualities onto plants. Mancuso doesn't care. He argues that the term neurobiology is provocative precisely because it forces us to reconsider what we mean by intelligence — if a plant can learn without a brain, what does that tell us about the relationship between brains and intelligence? Maybe intelligence requires something else, something we haven't named yet.

The idea that plants are low-level organisms dates back to Aristotle, Mancuso has said. The great philosopher believed that plants only possess a vegetative soul, as they lack movement and therefore do not need to sense. That they are on the edge between living and not living. Today we know that plants do not simply survive, but are highly complex living organisms with an ability to sense, far more sophisticated in sensing than animals, and able to communicate. Plants are extraordinary communicators: they communicate with other plants, distinguish kin from non-kin, communicate with plants of other species, communicate with animals by producing chemical volatiles during pollination. They can count, make decisions, learn from experience, remember. And they communicate underground, through a network of fungi called mycorrhizae, the fungi connecting the roots of different trees, forming a web, a network. Some scientists call it the wood wide web, and through this web trees share nutrients, send chemical signals, warn each other about insect attacks. A tree under siege by caterpillars can send a chemical signal through the fungal network, and neighboring trees will increase their chemical defenses before the caterpillars even arrive.

Trees share food through the network: a mother tree can send carbon to a shaded seedling, a dying tree can dump its resources into the network for others to use. The forest is a community, a superorganism. Aristotle said plants were on the edge between living and not living. He was wrong. Plants are at the center of a network that connects them to each other and to the soil and to the fungi and to the insects and to the entire ecosystem. They are alive in ways we are only beginning to understand.

And now the word vegetable comes full circle. The word was built on Aristotle's hierarchy — the word vegetative meant the lowest level, the level of growth without thought, of life without consciousness. But the science has demolished the hierarchy. Plants are not less alive, they are alive differently, intelligent in ways we don't fully understand. The word carries a hierarchy that science has proved wrong: every time we say vegetative, we are using a word that assumes plants are simple, passive, less. The science says otherwise. The word remembers what Aristotle forgot: that life is not a hierarchy. It is a network. And plants are part of it.
