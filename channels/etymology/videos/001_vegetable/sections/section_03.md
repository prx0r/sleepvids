
# SECTION 2: THE ROOT AND THE ROOT FAMILY

The story of vegetable begins with a sound. *weg-. A consonant cluster followed by a short vowel and a hard g. Proto-Indo-European — the reconstructed ancestor language spoken perhaps five thousand years ago, perhaps more, by people who left no written record. We know it only because their words left traces in hundreds of languages that came after.

The root *weg- meant to be strong, to be lively, to be active — vigorously alive, the kind of alive that does things, that moves, that acts. From this root, the Proto-Italic people derived a verb, vegere, meaning to be alive, to be active, to quicken, to make something come alive. From vegere came an adjective, vegetus — a quality, a state of being, something that described vigor and enlivenment. Vegetus meant vigorous, enlivened, active, sprightly. Think of the word vibrant, think of the word alive at its most energetic. That was vegetus.

From vegetus came a verb, vegetare — to enliven, to animate, to give something the quality of vegetus. To vegetate meant to make something alive, to fill it with vitality, the exact opposite of what it means today. From vegetare came an adjective, vegetabilis. In Late Latin, this meant animating, enlivening — something that has the power to enliven, something that gives life. In Medieval Latin, the word shifted slightly, vegetabilis came to mean growing, flourishing, still about the fundamental power of life itself, the power to grow, the power to become.

And then Old French got hold of it. Vegetable in Old French meant living, fit to live. The word traveled from Latin into the Romance languages, carrying its meaning of vitality and growth. Picture the word traveling: from Rome to Gaul, from Latin to Old French. As the Roman Empire collapsed and the languages broke apart, the word broke apart too, losing some of its precision, gaining some of its Romance warmth. Vegetable in Old French was softer than vegetabilis in Latin, less philosophical, more human — living, fit to live, a lived experience rather than an abstraction.

The word spent about five hundred years in Old French, five centuries of spoken language, five centuries of mouths shaping it. From the fall of Rome to the Norman Conquest. And then, in 1066, the Normans conquered England, brought their French with them, and the word came too.

And then, in the early fifteenth century, it entered English. Vegetable — first as an adjective, around 1400. The dictionary says it meant capable of life or growth, growing, vigorous, of material substances, of the plant kingdom, living and growing as a plant. The word described a quality of existence, a category on a grocery shelf.

By the mid-fifteenth century, vegetable had become a noun. It meant non-animal life, any plant — the ones you eat and the ones you don't. Trees were vegetables. Flowers were vegetables. The oak in the forest was a vegetable. The rose in the garden was a vegetable.

The Old English word it replaced was wyrt. We still have this word in St. John's wort and liverwort. Wyrt meant root, herb, vegetable, plant, spice — a broad word for anything that grew. Vegetable displaced it, the Latin-origin word won, and the same PIE root that gave us vegetable also gave us vigour, vigil, wake, watch, witch, Wicca, wicked, velocity, surveillance, reveille, and bivouac — all from *weg-, all from to be strong, be lively.

Vegetable is the one word from this family that lost its power. It went from meaning the power to be alive to meaning the thing you push around your plate, from to be strong to broccoli. The other words kept their strength — vigour is still vigorous, wake is still awake, watch is still watching, vigil is still alert. Even witch and wicked, which took dark turns, kept their energy. A witch is still powerful, wicked still means intense. But vegetable went from strong to edible to dull to brain-dead. It's the word in the family that lost its spine.

Let's spend a moment with each of the major cognates, because they tell us what the root originally meant, before vegetable forgot. Vigour, from Latin vigor, meaning liveliness, activity, force — we still use this word to mean energy, vitality, a person of great vigour is a person of great energy, and the word has not forgotten its roots. Wake, from Old English wacan, to become awake, to arise, to be born, to originate — and from Old English wacian, to be or remain awake. The same root, the same meaning. To be awake is to be alive. To be alive is to be strong.

Watch, from Old English wæccan, meaning to keep watch, to be awake — essentially the same word as wake. A watcher is someone who stays awake, who remains alert, who does not sleep when others do. In medieval cities, there were actual night watchmen, men who walked the streets after dark, carrying lanterns, calling out the hours, watching for fires, for criminals, for trouble. They were the city's eyes through the night. Their job was to stay awake while everyone else slept — to watch, to be the city's vigilance. The word watch comes from their job. To watch is to be awake when others are not, to remain alert when the world is dark.

Vigil, from Latin vigilia, meaning a watch, watchfulness — the state of being awake when you should be sleeping. An Irish wake was a vigil for the dead: the body lay in the house, the family sat with it through the night, they stayed awake, they told stories, they kept the dead company. A wake was a watching, an act of staying alive in the presence of death. From the root that means to be strong, to be lively. The word wake carries both meanings — to wake up, and to keep watch with the dead. The same word, the same root. To be alive and to sit with death.

Witch, from Old English wicce, means a female magician or sorceress — the etymologist Watkins says the word represents a Proto-Germanic *wikkjaz, a necromancer, one who wakes the dead. A witch is someone who has power over life and death, someone who can make the dead rise, from the root that means to be strong, to be lively. In the Laws of Ælfred, written around 890, witchcraft was singled out as a woman's craft, and the Old English text says: Ða fæmnan þe gewuniað onfon gealdorcræftigan & scinlæcan & wiccan, ne læt þu ða libban. The women who are accustomed to receive enchanters and sorceresses and witches, do not let them live. The word wicca renders Latin augur in Anglo-Saxon glossaries, and wicce stands for pythoness, divinatricem — a woman who speaks for the gods, a woman who sees the future. In the Three Kings of Cologne, from around 1400, wicca translates Magi — the wise men, the ones who knew.

Wicca, the modern pagan religion, was named from the Old English word for witch, from the root that means to be strong, to be alive. Wicked, from the same root, originally meant witch-like, then evil, then in modern slang, excellent and intense — a wicked guitar solo, a wicked sunset. The word went from dark to powerful, keeping its energy. Surveillance, French, from sur- over plus veiller to watch, from Latin vigilare, from vigil — watchful, to watch over someone, to keep them under observation. Velocity, from Latin velox, meaning fast, lively — speed that comes from being alive. Reveille, French, the morning call, the signal to wake up, from réveil, awakening. Bivouac, from Swiss German, a temporary military camp, soldiers who stay awake through the night.

All of these words come from the same place, all of them carrying the original meaning of *weg- — strength, aliveness, alertness. And then there's vegetable, the word that went from to be strong to the thing you hide in your mashed potatoes.

How did one word from this family lose everything its siblings kept, how did vegetable forget what it meant to be vigorous while vigour remembered, how did it forget what it meant to be awake while wake remembered? The answer is that the word traveled through too many hands, too many languages, too many centuries, too many people who needed it to mean different things. Each person who used the word gave it a new shade, and over five thousand years the shades accumulated, until the word meant something its original speakers would not recognize.
