
# SECTION 4: ARISTOTLE AND THE MEDIEVAL WORD

To understand why the word changed, we need to understand the man who made it change: Aristotle, 384 to 322 BCE, born in Macedonia, taught in Athens, the philosopher who classified everything. But before he was a philosopher, Aristotle was a biologist. He spent years on the island of Lesbos, studying marine life. He dissected squid, cataloged fish, watched how octopuses moved. He was fascinated by the difference between things that were alive and things that were not. What makes a fish a fish? What makes a plant a plant? What makes a human a human?

He founded a school in Athens called the Lyceum, where the students walked in a covered walkway called the peripatos — the walkers. Aristotle taught while walking, talked while moving, his philosophy restless, curious, always probing. He was also the tutor of Alexander the Great. Imagine that: the boy who would conquer the known world, sitting at the feet of the man who would classify it. Alexander reportedly sent specimens back to Aristotle from his campaigns, plants and animals from the edges of the known world, the philosopher's curiosity fed by the conqueror's empire.

Aristotle wrote many books, but one of them changed how Western civilization thought about life itself. It was called De Anima — On the Soul — written around 350 BCE, one of the most influential books in Western history. In it, Aristotle asked a simple question: what makes something alive?

His answer was the soul — Aristotle's soul was a set of capacities, a way of asking what can this thing do, what can it become, what makes it alive rather than dead. Aristotle wrote: Now given that there are bodies of such and such a kind, viz. having life, the soul cannot be a body; for the body is the subject or matter, not what is attributed to it. Hence the soul must a substance in the sense of the form of a natural body having life potentially within it.

The soul, Aristotle says, is like the sharpness of an axe — the quality that makes the axe an axe. Cut away the sharpness and you have metal but not an axe. Cut away the soul and you have a body but not a living thing. He uses another analogy: the eye. What is an eye? It's a thing that sees. If an eye were made of stone and couldn't see, it would be an eye in name only, having the shape of an eye but having lost its soul.

And then he goes further: what about appetite? Appetite is the soul's desire for food, and plants have it, animals have it, humans have it — it's the most basic desire, the desire to continue existing, to take in matter and make it part of yourself, to grow, to persist. Aristotle says that appetite is common to all living things, the vegetative soul at work, the most fundamental level of existence, the desire to be alive.

This is strange and beautiful: appetite — hunger, desire, the need to eat — is a vegetative thing, coming from the lowest level of the soul, the level that plants share with us. When you feel hunger, you're feeling what a tree feels when it reaches toward the sun. The same desire, the same drive, the same root. The soul isn't hiding somewhere inside you, waiting to float away when you die. The soul is what you are, the organization of your body, the pattern that makes you alive rather than dead. When the pattern breaks, the soul is gone — it no longer exists.

Aristotle takes it for granted that to have a soul is to be alive, and by life he means self-nutrition and growth and decay — the things that living things do, growing, feeding, reproducing, dying. If a thing does these things, it has a soul. Even plants. So even plants have a kind of soul — a psuchē, the Greek word we get psyche from. But humans have a more complex soul, and this is where Aristotle builds his hierarchy. Broadly speaking, there are three classes of capacities of living things, and Aristotle arranges these hierarchically: higher-order living things possess all the capacities of lower-level living things.

First, nutrition and generation: plants and animals both have this, they can take in food, they can grow, they can make more of themselves. This is the vegetative soul, the nutritive soul, the lowest level. Second, perception: all animals have this, they can see, hear, smell, taste, touch, they can move toward food and away from danger, they can feel pleasure and pain. Animals have everything plants have, plus perception — the sensitive soul. Third, thinking, understanding, reason: humans have this, we can think, we can abstract, we can contemplate the eternal. We have everything animals have, plus reason — the rational soul.

This is Aristotle's great chain of being: plants at the bottom, animals in the middle, humans at the top, and above humans, if you wanted, the divine. The key insight was this: each higher level contained all the levels below it. Humans didn't just have reason — we also had perception, and we also had nutrition and growth. We were everything a plant was, plus everything an animal was, plus reason. The hierarchy was additive. The soul of an apple tree brings apple tree capacities, the soul of a dog dog capacities, the soul of a human human capacities. The exercise of these capacities makes us fully alive and fully who we are.

This framework dominated Western thought for two thousand years, through the Roman period, through the early Christian period, through the medieval period. Thomas Aquinas, the greatest of the medieval theologians, adopted Aristotle's classification wholesale. Albertus Magnus, the teacher of Aquinas, did the same.

Here's how it happened: when the Roman Empire fell, Aristotle's works were lost to Western Europe. They survived in the Arab world, where Islamic scholars — Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd — translated Aristotle into Arabic, commented on him, debated him, and kept his ideas alive while Europe forgot them. Then, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Aristotle's works were translated from Arabic into Latin, arriving in Europe like a flood. Suddenly, the greatest philosopher of antiquity was available again, and the medieval theologians had to reckon with him.

Albertus Magnus, a German Dominican friar, took on the task, writing commentaries on Aristotle and trying to reconcile Aristotle's pagan philosophy with Christian theology. He classified plants and animals using Aristotle's system, and he was one of the first Europeans to study botany systematically. His student, Thomas Aquinas, went further: he built an entire theological system on Aristotle's foundations, arguing that faith and reason were not enemies, that Aristotle's logic could serve Christianity, that the pagan philosopher and the Christian saint were walking the same road.

And in this synthesis, the vegetative soul took on new meaning — it was a theological category, the soul that God gave to plants, the soul that managed the involuntary functions, the soul that kept the body alive without thought or feeling. To be vegetative was to have what God gave to plants, the hierarchy now divine, built into the fabric of creation. To be vegetable was to be at the bottom of God's plan.

This is the weight the word carried when it entered English — a divine hierarchy, a cosmic order. The vegetative was lower, the vegetative was less, the vegetative was not enough. The vegetative soul became a standard category in medieval philosophy and theology — the soul that made the body alive, the soul that managed the involuntary functions: growth, nutrition, reproduction, the things your body does without thinking about them, the things plants do. The medievals used the word vegetativus, from Medieval Latin vegetativus, from vegetat-, the past participle stem of vegetare — the power to enliven, the power to make alive.

But here's where it gets interesting: the medievals also used the word to describe a hierarchy of consciousness. Plants had the vegetative soul, alive but not conscious. Animals had the sensitive soul, conscious but not rational. Humans had the rational soul, conscious and rational. This hierarchy was moral, spiritual, the vegetative was lower, the rational was higher. To be vegetable was to be less, to be human was to be more.

And this is where the word began to change — in value. To be called vegetative was a judgment, a statement that you were at the bottom of the hierarchy, alive but barely, growing but not feeling, existing but not thinking. The word carried this moral weight for centuries, through the Renaissance, through the Reformation, through the Enlightenment, each era adding its own layer. The Enlightenment valued reason above all else, and to be vegetable was to lack reason, to be less than human.

Cockeram's English Dictionarie, published in 1623, defined vegetive as Which liveth as plants do — the judgment was already there, living as plants do, not as humans do, the hierarchy embedded in the definition. Monasteries were the word's home, where monks kept the Latin alive and used vegetabilis in their texts and to classify, and they used it in their gardens. Every monastery had a garden — the hortus, the place where things grew — and in the hortus the monks grew herbs for medicine, food for the table, flowers for the altar, and they used the word vegetable to describe what they grew. But the monks also inherited Aristotle's hierarchy: they knew that plants were below animals in the chain of being, that the vegetative soul was the lowest level, and this knowledge shaped how they used the word. To call something vegetable was to place it in the hierarchy, to say it was alive but not as alive as an animal, a human, an angel. The word carried the entire weight of medieval cosmology.

By the time the word entered English, in the early fifteenth century, it carried all of this — it meant alive, but it meant alive at the bottom of the hierarchy, alive but less, alive but not enough. The word was waiting for what would happen next: the narrowing, the decline, the moment when alive would become food, and food would become dull, and dull would become brain-dead. Aristotle built the hierarchy, the monks maintained it, the Enlightenment reinforced it, and medicine completed it.

The word vegetative traveled from Aristotle's Lyceum to a modern hospital room, a journey of twenty-three hundred years. And every step of the way, it carried the same judgment: the vegetative is lower, the vegetative is less, the vegetative is not enough. Until the science proved otherwise. But we'll get to that.

For now, let the word settle: vegetable, a word that once meant the power to grow, now means the loss of everything that makes us human. Aristotle's ghost, walking through a hospital corridor, his classification naming the unthinkable.
